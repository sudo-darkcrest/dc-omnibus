Defending the Organization: Security Architecture, Operations and Response

Why this matters. Module 17 named the attacks; this module is the defender's job: designing networks that contain damage, hardening systems, watching logs, responding when something goes wrong and managing risk so leadership can make decisions. Security+ Domains 3, 4 and 5 together are about two thirds of the exam, and the same ideas reappear in CISM (Certified Information Security Manager) and CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor). The module ends with a 90-question Security+ readiness exam covering Module 16 to 16.

You will be able to

  • Design segmented networks with DMZs (Demilitarized Zone), firewalls, IDS/IPS (IDS: Intrusion Detection System; IPS: Intrusion Prevention System), NAC (Network Access Control), jump servers and SASE (Secure Access Service Edge).
  • Harden endpoints, mobile devices, wireless and applications with baselines and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response).
  • Use logs, SIEM (Security Information and Event Management), SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation and Response) and threat intelligence to detect and investigate attacks.
  • Run the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-61 incident response lifecycle and preserve digital evidence properly.
  • Plan continuity (BIA (Business Impact Analysis), RTO (Recovery Time Objective), RPO (Recovery Point Objective), sites, backups) and manage risk with registers, matrices and treatments.

Device placement and failure modes

  • Inline vs tap/monitor: inline devices (IPS) can block but add a point of failure; taps/SPAN (SPAN: Switched Port Analyzer) ports (IDS) only watch.
  • Fail-open keeps traffic flowing if the device fails (availability); fail-closed blocks everything (security).
  • Specialized systems: ICS/SCADA (ICS: Industrial Control System; SCADA: Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition; industrial control), IoT (Internet of Things), embedded and RTOS (Real-Time Operating System) devices often can't be patched; isolate them on separate segments and monitor.
  • Resilience: high availability (clustering, load balancing), redundancy (power, links), multi-cloud, geographic dispersion.

2. Hardening endpoints, mobile, wireless and apps

Systems

  • Secure baselines: establish, deploy, maintain. Sources: CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmarks, vendor guides, DISA (Defense Information Systems Agency) STIGs (Security Technical Implementation Guide).
  • Disable unused services and ports; change default passwords; remove bloatware.
  • EDR (endpoint detection and response) watches behavior and can isolate a host; XDR (Extended Detection and Response) correlates across endpoint, network, email and cloud.
  • Host-based firewall, HIPS (Host-based Intrusion Prevention System), full-disk encryption, application allow lists (only approved software runs).
  • Patch management with testing and rollback.

Mobile and wireless

  • MDM (Mobile Device Management): enforce PINs (Personal Identification Number), encryption, remote wipe, app control, containerization.
  • Deployment models: BYOD (Bring Your Own Device) (bring your own), COPE (corporate-owned, personally enabled), CYOD (Choose Your Own Device) (choose your own from a list), corporate-owned only.
  • Wireless: WPA3 (Wi-Fi Protected Access 3), 802.1X/RADIUS for enterprise, site surveys and heat maps, WIPS (Wireless Intrusion Prevention System).

Applications

  • Secure coding: input validation (server-side), output encoding, secure cookies, error handling.
  • Static (SAST) and dynamic (DAST) analysis, sandboxing, code signing.

Data protection

  • Classification: e.g., public, internal/private, confidential/sensitive, restricted/critical (government: unclassified, confidential, secret, top secret).
  • DLP (Data Loss Prevention) watches data at rest, in transit and in use to block unauthorized transfers.
  • Methods: encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, obfuscation, segmentation, permission restrictions.
  • Data sovereignty: data is subject to the laws of the country where it is stored. Residency: requirements for where data must physically remain.

4. Incident response and forensics

  • Preparation: IR plan, team and contacts, tools, playbooks, training and tabletop exercises (discussion-based walkthroughs) and simulations.
  • Detection and analysis: alerts, triage, scoping, deciding it's an incident, documenting everything.
  • Containment: stop spread (isolate hosts, disable accounts, block IPs), short-term then long-term. Eradication: remove malware and the root cause. Recovery: restore, monitor, return to normal.
  • Post-incident activity / lessons learned: what happened, what worked, what to change; update plans. Also root cause analysis.

Digital forensics

Order of volatility (collect most volatile first)

  1. CPU (Central Processing Unit) registers and cache
  2. Routing table, ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) cache, process table, kernel stats, RAM (Random Access Memory)
  3. Temporary file systems / swap
  4. Disk
  5. Remote logging and monitoring data
  6. Physical configuration, network topology
  7. Archival media (backups)

From RFC (Request for Comments) 3227.

Preserve the evidence

  • Chain of custody: documented record of who handled the evidence, when and why.
  • Legal hold: instruction to preserve relevant data; overrides normal deletion.
  • Acquisition: forensic images with write blockers; verify with hashes.
  • E-discovery: finding and producing electronic information for legal proceedings.
  • Reporting, preservation and non-repudiation of findings.

6. Governance and risk at Security+ depth

Document hierarchy

  • Policy: high-level management intent ("all data must be classified"). Mandatory.
  • Standard: specific mandatory requirements ("AES-256 (Advanced Encryption Standard) for data at rest").
  • Procedure: step-by-step instructions.
  • Guideline: recommended, optional advice.

Common policies: AUP (Acceptable Use Policy), information security, BC/DR, incident response, SDLC (System Development Life Cycle), change management.

Risk management

  • Risk = likelihood × impact. Qualitative (high/medium/low) or quantitative: SLE = asset value × exposure factor; ARO = yearly occurrences; ALE = SLE × ARO.
  • Risk register: list of risks, owners, scores, treatments, status.
  • Appetite (how much risk the organization is willing to pursue) vs tolerance (acceptable variation) vs threshold.
  • Treatments: accept, avoid (stop the activity), transfer (insurance, outsourcing), mitigate (controls). Exemptions and exceptions are formal acceptance.
  • Inherent risk (before controls) vs residual risk (after).

Third parties and compliance

  • Vendor assessment: right-to-audit clauses, questionnaires, independent assessments, supply chain analysis, monitoring.
  • Agreements: SLA (Service Level Agreement), MOA/MOU (MOA: Memorandum of Agreement; MOU: Memorandum of Understanding), MSA (Master Service Agreement), SOW (Statement of Work), NDA (Non-Disclosure Agreement), BPA (Blanket Purchase Agreement).
  • Compliance: reporting, consequences (fines, sanctions, loss of licence), attestation, audits (internal and external).

Privacy and people

  • Privacy law in general terms: GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation; EU (European Union)) defines data subjects, controllers (decide purpose) and processors (process on the controller's behalf), and the right to be forgotten. Many other national and state laws exist (verify which apply to you).
  • Security awareness training: phishing campaigns, anomalous behavior recognition, reporting, measuring results.

7. An incident response exercise you can run

Plans that are never practised fail when they are needed. A tabletop exercise is a discussion where the team walks through a made-up incident. Here is a short scenario you can use.

TimeNew information ("inject")Questions for the team
09:00The help desk gets three calls: files on a shared drive have strange extensions and won't openWho is told first? Is this an incident? Who declares it?
09:20EDR shows the same suspicious program on 12 machinesDo we isolate the machines? Who has authority to cut network access?
10:00A ransom note appears; payroll runs tomorrowWhich systems are restored first? Are backups clean? Do we involve legal counsel, insurers and law enforcement?
11:30A journalist calls asking about a breachWho speaks for the company? What do we tell staff and customers?
Day 3Systems are restoredWhat evidence did we keep? What notification deadlines apply? What do we change?

Afterwards, write down the gaps you found (missing phone numbers, unclear authority, untested backups) and fix them. That is the "lessons learned" step that closes the incident response lifecycle.

Keep a printed copy of the incident plan and contact list. During a ransomware attack, the shared drive holding the plan may be encrypted too.

8. A practical hardening checklist

Hardening means reducing the ways a system can be attacked. This checklist applies to almost any server, laptop or network device.

  • Patch the OS, firmware and apps on a regular schedule; patch known-exploited vulnerabilities urgently.
  • Remove unused software, services, accounts and open ports.
  • Change defaults: passwords, admin usernames, SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) community strings.
  • Enforce MFA for all admin access and remote access.
  • Least privilege: users are not local administrators; admins use separate admin accounts.
  • Encrypt disks and use secure protocols (SSH (Secure Shell) not Telnet, HTTPS not HTTP, SFTP (Secure Shell File Transfer Protocol) not FTP (File Transfer Protocol)).
  • Turn on logging and send logs to a central system.
  • Use a baseline (such as CIS Benchmarks or vendor guides) and check for drift automatically.
  • Back up configurations and data, and test restores.
Hardening is like securing a house: lock the doors you use, brick up the ones you don't, take the spare key out from under the mat, and install an alarm.

Knowledge check

Security+ readiness exam (90 questions, Modules 16 to 18)

Questions come from the Security+ tagged items in Modules 16 to 18 plus an extra Security+ bank, weighted and scored by the five SY0-701 domains: General Security Concepts (12%), Threats, Vulnerabilities and Mitigations (22%), Security Architecture (18%), Security Operations (28%) and Security Program Management and Oversight (20%). The real exam has up to 90 questions (including performance-based) in 90 minutes, with a passing score of 750 on a 100 to 900 scale. CompTIA updates exams roughly every three years, so verify the current version before you book.
