Support Like a Pro: Troubleshooting, Virtualization and Operational Procedures

Why this matters. Knowing parts and commands is not enough: support work is judged on how you troubleshoot, document, communicate and follow procedure. A+ dedicates whole domains to troubleshooting (Core 1) and operational procedures (Core 2), and virtualization and cloud now appear on Core 1. This module ties Part 2 together and ends with a full A+ readiness exam.

You will be able to

  • Apply the troubleshooting method to hardware, storage, video, mobile, printer and network problems.
  • Explain Type 1 and Type 2 hypervisors, VMs (Virtual Machine), snapshots and the cloud service and deployment models.
  • Follow operational procedures: ticketing, documentation, asset and change management.
  • Recognize script file types and their risks, and choose secure remote access tools.
  • Handle safety, privacy, licensing, prohibited content and professional communication correctly.
  • Choose the right data destruction method and evidence of destruction.

2. Virtualization and cloud basics

A hypervisor (virtual machine monitor) creates and runs virtual machines (VMs): software computers with virtual CPUs, RAM, disks and NICs (Network Interface Card).

Type 1 (bare metal)

Runs directly on the hardware. Efficient and secure; used in data centers. Examples: VMware ESXi, Microsoft Hyper-V, KVM (Keyboard, Video, Mouse), Xen, Proxmox.

Type 2 (hosted)

Runs as an app on a normal OS (Operating System). Convenient for desktops, labs and testing. Examples: VirtualBox, VMware Workstation/Fusion, Parallels.

  • Snapshots: save a VM's state at a point in time so you can roll back after a bad update. Not a backup: they usually depend on the original disk.
  • Resource allocation: assign vCPUs (Virtual Central Processing Unit), RAM, disk and virtual NICs. The CPU must support virtualization (VT-x/AMD-V (VT: Virtualization Technology) enabled in UEFI (Unified Extensible Firmware Interface)).
  • Sandbox: an isolated environment for testing software or opening suspicious files; Windows Sandbox is a built-in example.
  • Uses: test/development, running legacy OSs, consolidating servers, cross-platform apps. Security risk: VM escape, VM sprawl (unmanaged VMs).

Cloud computing at A+ depth

Deployment models: public, private, hybrid, community. Characteristics: shared resources, metered utilization (pay per use), rapid elasticity, high availability, file synchronization. Desktop virtualization (VDI) and cloud desktops deliver Windows to thin clients.

3. Operational procedures

Ticketing and documentation

  • A good ticket records: user and contact info, device/asset, clear problem description, category, severity/priority, steps taken, escalation, resolution and closure notes written in plain language.
  • Documentation types: standard operating procedures (SOPs), knowledge base articles, network diagrams, incident reports, acceptable use policy (AUP), regulatory compliance requirements, new user setup and offboarding checklists.

Asset management

An asset inventory tracks each device: asset tag, serial, assigned user, location, purchase date, warranty, license and lifecycle stage (procurement → deployment → maintenance → disposal). A database (CMDB) links assets to services.

Change management basics

  1. Request with purpose, scope and the business reason.
  2. Risk analysis: what could break, how likely, how bad.
  3. Plan: implementation steps, test results (sandbox), schedule (maintenance window), affected systems and users.
  4. Backout (rollback) plan: how to undo if it fails.
  5. Approval by the change advisory board (CAB) or change authority.
  6. Implement, then communicate and document; get end-user acceptance.

Safety and environmental controls

  • Temperature, humidity (around 40 to 60% relative humidity limits both ESD (electrostatic discharge) and condensation), ventilation, dust control (compressed air, vacuums made for electronics).
  • Power: surge protectors, UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply), battery backup for critical gear.
  • Follow local government regulations for disposal; equipment placement and grounding.

Privacy, licensing and prohibited content

  • Licensing: valid licenses, DRM (Digital Rights Management), personal vs corporate use, open source terms, license compliance.
  • Regulated data: PII (personally identifiable information), PHI (Protected Health Information; health), PCI (payment card), government data; follow data retention requirements.
  • Incident response for prohibited content (illegal material or policy violations found on a device): identify it, report it through proper channels (manager, legal, law enforcement per policy), preserve the data/device, keep a documented chain of custody. Do not delete, copy or share it yourself.

Professionalism and communication

  • Be on time, dress appropriately, use proper language (no jargon or slang), maintain a positive attitude, actively listen and do not interrupt, be culturally sensitive.
  • Avoid distractions (personal calls, social media) and do not argue with customers or minimize their problems.
  • Set and meet expectations, give options, follow up afterward, keep confidential information private (do not read their files or documents).

4. Data destruction and disposal

MethodHowReuse possible?Notes
Standard formatRebuilds the file systemYesData is often recoverable. Not sanitization.
Overwrite / wipeWrites zeros/patterns over every sectorYesFine for HDDs; for SSDs use the drive's Secure Erase or cryptographic erase because of wear leveling.
DegaussingStrong magnetic field erases magnetic mediaNo (HDD becomes unusable)Does not work on SSDs or optical media.
Physical destructionShredding, drilling, crushing, incinerationNoShredding is required for the highest classifications.
Third-party vendorCertified destruction serviceNoObtain a certificate of destruction listing serial numbers.
NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-88 defines three levels of media sanitization: Clear (overwrite, protects against simple recovery), Purge (secure erase, crypto erase, degauss: protects against lab recovery) and Destroy (shred, disintegrate, incinerate).

5. Writing a good ticket

A ticket is the record of a problem and everything done about it. Good tickets make you look professional, help the next technician, and feed the metrics managers use (Module 31). Compare these two.

Weak ticketStrong ticket
"Fixed printer."Problem: Front desk printer HP-02 showed "offline" for 3 users since 9:10 AM.
Cause: Printer had picked up a new IP address after a power cut; the queue pointed at the old one.
Fix: Reserved 192.168.1.50 in the router's DHCP settings and updated the print queue.
Verified: Test page printed from two PCs (Personal Computer).
Prevention: Added reservations for the other two printers.

The parts every ticket needs

  • Who and what: user, device, location, how many people are affected.
  • Priority: based on impact (how many people) and urgency (how soon it hurts the business).
  • Steps taken: including the things that did not work, so nobody repeats them.
  • Resolution and verification: what fixed it and how you proved it.
  • Customer confirmation: close the ticket only after the user agrees it is fixed.
Write every ticket as if the user's manager will read it. Stick to facts, avoid blame, and never paste passwords into a ticket.

6. The troubleshooting method in action

CompTIA's six-step method is easy to memorise and easy to forget under pressure. Here it is applied to one call: "My laptop won't connect to Wi-Fi."

StepWhat the tech does
1. Identify the problemAsks questions: did it ever work here? Anything changed? Do other devices connect? Learns a Windows update installed last night.
2. Establish a theory of probable causeStarts simple: the update may have changed the Wi-Fi driver.
3. Test the theoryDevice Manager shows a warning on the Wi-Fi adapter. Theory confirmed. (If not, form a new theory or escalate.)
4. Plan and implement the fixRolls back the driver, after checking the change policy allows it.
5. Verify full functionalityLaptop connects; the tech also checks the VPN and printer work. Pauses that driver update.
6. DocumentWrites the ticket, and flags the bad driver so other laptops can be protected.
The exam loves the word FIRST. Before changing anything, the first step is always to identify the problem by asking questions and backing up data if there is any risk.

Key terms

Memory aids

Hypervisors: Type 1 = 1st on the metal. Type 2 = 2nd, on top of an OS.
Cloud: IaaS = I manage the OS; PaaS = Provider runs the platform; SaaS = Simply use it.
Prohibited content: Identify, Report, Preserve, Chain of custody → "I Really Prefer Custody".
Sanitization: Clear, Purge, Destroy → "Can't Possibly Dig it up".
Exam trapWhy it is wrong
"Degauss the SSD."Degaussing only affects magnetic media; use secure/crypto erase or shredding.
"A snapshot is a backup."Snapshots depend on the base disk and live on the same storage.
"Delete the illegal files you found."Preserve evidence and report; deleting destroys the chain of custody.
"Open RDP to the internet for convenience."Exposed RDP is a top ransomware entry point; use a VPN/gateway with MFA.
"Skip the rollback plan for small changes."Every change needs a backout plan.
