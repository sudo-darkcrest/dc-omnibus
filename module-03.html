How We Got Here: A History of Computing, Networking and the Internet

Why this matters. Today's technology is a stack of old decisions. IPv4 addresses run out because of a choice made in 1981. The Ethernet frame still carries fields from 1980. Passwords are hashed because of breaches in the 1980s and 2010s. Knowing the history explains why things are the way they are, makes odd exam facts memorable, and shows you where the next changes are likely to come from.

You will be able to

  • Place the key inventions in computing, networking and storage on a timeline with the people and organizations behind them.
  • Explain the generations of computers: relays and vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits and microprocessors.
  • Explain Moore's law and Dennard scaling, and where they stand today.
  • Describe how ARPANET became the internet and how the web, Wi-Fi and broadband followed.
  • Connect famous security incidents to the practices and laws they changed.
  • Name the main standards bodies and roughly when each was formed.

Cert map

History is rarely tested directly, but it explains many tested facts (why IPv6 exists, why MD5 is broken, why FAT32 has a 4 GiB file limit).

1. Before electronics

  • Abacus (ancient Mesopotamia, China and Rome): beads on rods for arithmetic. The first "storage" of numbers outside the head.
  • Jacquard loom (Joseph Marie Jacquard, 1804): punched cards controlled the weaving pattern. The idea that a machine can follow a stored, changeable program starts here.
  • Charles Babbage: the Difference Engine (designed 1820s) to calculate tables mechanically, and the Analytical Engine (designed 1837), which had a "mill" (a processor), a "store" (memory), punched-card input and conditional branching. It was never finished, but its design is a general-purpose computer.
  • Ada Lovelace (1843) published notes on the Analytical Engine with an algorithm to compute Bernoulli numbers, often called the first published computer program, and saw that such machines could manipulate symbols, not only numbers.
  • Herman Hollerith built electromechanical punched-card tabulators for the 1890 US census, cutting processing from years to months. His company became part of IBM (1924).

3. Generations of computers

Relays and vacuum tubes (1940s to 1950s)

Early machines switched with electromechanical relays (Zuse Z3, 1941; Harvard Mark I, 1944) or vacuum tubes, glass bulbs that could switch current with no moving parts but ran hot and burned out. Colossus (UK, 1943 to 1944, Tommy Flowers) broke German Lorenz cipher traffic. ENIAC (US, completed 1945, Eckert and Mauchly) used about 17,500 tubes, weighed about 27 tonnes and drew about 150 kW. UNIVAC I (1951) was the first commercial computer in the US.

Transistors (1947 onward)

The transistor, a solid-state switch and amplifier, was invented at Bell Labs in 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley. Transistors were smaller, cooler, cheaper and far more reliable than tubes. Transistorized computers arrived in the late 1950s.

Integrated circuits (1958 to 1959)

An integrated circuit (IC) puts many transistors on one piece of semiconductor. Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments, 1958) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild, 1959, silicon planar process) invented it independently. ICs powered the IBM System/360 family's successors, the Apollo guidance computer and minicomputers.

Microprocessors (1971 onward)

A microprocessor is a whole CPU on one chip. The Intel 4004 (1971, about 2,300 transistors, 4-bit) started it, followed by the 8080, the 8086/8088 (the x86 family, 1978) and ARM (Acorn, 1985).

Personal computers, laptops and phones

Altair 8800 (1975) kits sparked the hobbyist PC market (and Microsoft's first product). Apple II (1977) brought color graphics and spreadsheets (VisiCalc). The IBM PC (1981) used open, off-the-shelf parts, so "PC compatibles" took over business computing. Laptops matured in the late 1980s and 1990s. The smartphone era began with the iPhone (2007) and Android (2008). Cloud computing went mainstream with Amazon Web Services (S3 and EC2, 2006). Since about 2012, GPUs and dedicated AI accelerators drive the biggest growth in data center compute.

4. Moore's law and Dennard scaling

Moore's law: in 1965 Gordon Moore (later an Intel co-founder) observed that the number of components on a chip was doubling every year; in 1975 he revised it to about every two years. It is an observation about economics and manufacturing, not a law of physics.

Dennard scaling (Robert Dennard, IBM, 1974): as transistors shrink, voltage and current shrink too, so power per unit area stays constant. That let clock speeds rise "for free" for decades.

Dennard scaling broke down around 2005 to 2007 because leakage current stopped falling with size. Clock speeds stalled near 3 to 5 GHz, so the industry moved to multiple cores, specialized accelerators (GPUs, NPUs) and smarter designs. Transistor counts kept growing through 3D transistors (FinFET, gate-all-around), chiplets and advanced packaging, though the doubling has slowed and the cost per transistor no longer falls as fast. Modern "3 nm" or "2 nm" names are marketing labels, not a physical gate length.

6. Networking and the internet

  • Telegraph (1830s to 1840s, Morse code) and telephone (1876, Bell): the first electrical networks. The phone network's circuit switching dominated for a century.
  • Packet switching (Paul Baran and Donald Davies, early to mid 1960s): break data into packets that travel independently, so the network survives broken links.
  • ARPANET (1969): first message between UCLA and SRI on 29 October 1969. Funded by the US DoD's ARPA.
  • Ethernet (1973, Xerox PARC, Robert Metcalfe and David Boggs); standardized as IEEE 802.3 in 1983.
  • TCP/IP (Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, 1974 paper): ARPANET switched to TCP/IP on 1 January 1983 ("flag day"), the birth of the internet as a network of networks.
  • DNS (1983 to 1987, Paul Mockapetris) replaced a single shared hosts file.
  • World Wide Web (Tim Berners-Lee at CERN: proposal 1989, first website 1991): HTML, HTTP and URLs on top of the internet. The web is not the internet; it is one service that runs on it.
  • Wi-Fi: IEEE 802.11 published in 1997 (2 Mbps); 802.11b (1999, 11 Mbps) and the Wi-Fi Alliance branding made it mainstream.
  • Broadband: DSL and cable modems (DOCSIS 1.0, 1997) in the late 1990s; fiber to the home (PON standards from the 2000s).
  • Cellular: 1G analog (1980s), 2G digital (GSM, 1991), 3G (2000s), 4G LTE (around 2009 to 2010), 5G (from 2019).

9. Data centers: from mainframe rooms to the edge

  1. Mainframe rooms (1950s to 1970s): one huge computer in a raised-floor room with dedicated cooling and power.
  2. Server rooms (1980s to 1990s): rooms of PC servers as client-server computing spread.
  3. Colocation (late 1990s, the dot-com boom): rent space, power and cooling in a shared facility; connect to many carriers in a meet-me room.
  4. Hyperscale (2000s onward): giant facilities run by cloud and internet companies, with custom servers and very efficient cooling (PUE near 1.1).
  5. Edge (2010s onward): small data centers close to users for low latency (5G, content delivery, IoT).
  6. AI-era (2020s): racks drawing 40 to over 100 kW, driving liquid cooling and new power designs (Module 18).

Exam / interview trapWhy it is wrong
"The internet and the web are the same."The internet (1983, TCP/IP) is the network; the web (1989 to 1991) is a service using HTTP on top of it.
