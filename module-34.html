Risk and IT Governance

Why this matters. CRISC (Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control) and CGEIT (Certified in the Governance of Enterprise IT) are both on the top-paying list. They are for people who decide which IT (Information Technology) risks are acceptable and whether IT spending is worth it. Even if you never sit them, understanding risk language and governance helps you explain technical problems to managers in terms they act on.

You will be able to

  • Describe the CRISC and CGEIT exams and experience requirements.
  • Define risk appetite, tolerance, capacity, inherent and residual risk, and choose good KRIs (Key Risk Indicator).
  • Build risk scenarios and analyse them qualitatively and quantitatively.
  • Select risk responses and controls, and explain the three lines model.
  • Explain governance versus management, COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies) EDM, business cases, portfolio management and benefits realization.

Cert map

1. CRISC and CGEIT: risk and the boardroom

ISACA (Information Systems Audit and Control Association), which offers CISM (Certified Information Security Manager; Module 35) and CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor; Module 36), also has two certifications on the top-paying list: CRISC (Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control) for IT risk professionals and CGEIT (Certified in the Governance of Enterprise IT) for senior leaders who make sure IT serves the business.

2. The language of risk

TermMeaning
Risk appetiteHow much risk the organisation is willing to take, overall, to reach its goals (set by the board)
Risk toleranceThe acceptable variation around the appetite for a specific objective (for example "no more than 4 hours of downtime a year")
Risk capacityThe most risk the organisation can absorb before it fails
Inherent riskRisk before any controls
Residual riskRisk left after controls; must be within appetite or escalated
Risk ownerThe accountable business person who decides how the risk is treated
Control ownerThe person responsible for operating a specific control
Risk registerThe living list of risks, owners, ratings, responses and status

Indicators

  • KRI (key risk indicator): an early warning that risk is rising, for example "number of unpatched critical servers".
  • KPI (key performance indicator): how well a process is performing, for example "percentage of changes completed on time".
  • KCI (key control indicator): whether a control is working, for example "percentage of access reviews completed".
Exam trap. A good KRI is leading (predicts trouble) and has a threshold that triggers action. "Number of breaches last year" is lagging.

3. Identifying and assessing IT risk

  1. Set the context: business objectives, scope, appetite and the frameworks in use.
  2. Identify risks using risk scenarios: a threat actor, an event, an asset, a time and the business impact ("A disgruntled admin deletes the customer database, stopping orders for two days").
  3. Analyse likelihood and impact:
    • Qualitative: high/medium/low ratings plotted on a heat map. Quick and easy to communicate, but subjective.
    • Quantitative: money and probabilities (ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy), Module 20), or models such as FAIR (Factor Analysis of Information Risk) that estimate ranges of loss.
  4. Evaluate against appetite and prioritise.

Frameworks you'll meet: ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 31000 (general risk management principles), ISO/IEC (IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 27005 (information security risk), NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-30 (risk assessments) and SP 800-37 (Risk Management Framework), COSO ERM (enterprise risk management) and COBIT (Module 32).

Inputs include the business impact analysis (Module 20), vulnerability scans, threat intelligence, audit findings, incident history and third-party risk assessments.

4. Responding to and reporting risk

  • Responses: accept (formally, by the risk owner, within appetite), mitigate (add controls), transfer / share (insurance, outsourcing with contracts) or avoid (stop the activity).
  • Control types by purpose: preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating and recovery. By nature: administrative (policies, training), technical (encryption, MFA (Multifactor Authentication)) and physical (locks, guards).
  • Control design and testing: is the control designed to address the risk, and is it operating effectively? Gather evidence; don't assume.
  • Action plans have owners, deadlines and budgets, tracked in the register.
  • Reporting: dashboards for executives show top risks, trends, KRIs breaching thresholds and overdue actions. Tailor detail to the audience.

The three lines model

LineWhoRole
FirstBusiness and IT operationsOwn and manage risks and run controls day to day
SecondRisk management, compliance, security functionsSet frameworks, advise, monitor and challenge
ThirdInternal auditIndependent assurance to the board (Module 36)
On ISACA exams, the risk practitioner advises and facilitates. The risk owner (business) decides and accepts. If an answer has the risk practitioner accepting risk, it's usually wrong.

5. Governance of enterprise IT (CGEIT)

Governance is the board's job: setting direction and checking results. Management plans, builds, runs and monitors within that direction. COBIT expresses governance as EDM: Evaluate options and needs, Direct through priorities and decisions, and Monitor performance and compliance.

  • Strategic alignment: IT strategy follows from business strategy. An IT steering committee of business and IT leaders prioritises investments.
  • Governance framework: decision rights (who decides what), policies, organisational structures, culture and information flows.
  • Stakeholders: the board, executives, regulators, customers and staff have different needs; governance balances them.
  • Enterprise architecture (for example TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework)) gives a target picture so projects fit together.

IT resources

Make sure the right people, skills, infrastructure, applications, information and suppliers are available. Decide what to build, buy or outsource, and manage vendors through contracts and performance reviews.

6. Benefits realization and risk optimization

Getting value from IT spending

  • Business case: costs, benefits, risks, options and how benefits will be measured. It should be updated and revisited throughout the project, not filed away.
  • Portfolio management: treat IT investments like an investment portfolio. Balance new initiatives against keeping the lights on, and stop projects that no longer deliver value.
  • Benefits realization: assign benefit owners, track benefits after go-live, and compare with the business case.
  • Measures: return on investment (ROI), net present value (NPV), total cost of ownership (TCO) and the balanced scorecard (financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth perspectives).

Risk optimization

For CGEIT, risk isn't only something to minimise. The board should take enough risk to achieve goals, within appetite, and ensure IT risk is part of enterprise risk management rather than a separate silo.

Analogy. CRISC is the ship's navigator, constantly measuring dangers and recommending course changes. CGEIT is the ship's owner and captain deciding where the voyage should go and whether it is worth the fuel.

7. Preparing for ISACA's management exams

  • Think like a business-focused advisor: the best answer supports business objectives, follows a framework, and assigns accountability to the right owner.
  • Look for the first or best step: usually understanding business objectives, context or the risk itself before choosing controls.
  • Use ISACA's official review manual and question database; ISACA's own wording matters.
  • Natural order: CISA or CISM first for many people, then CRISC; CGEIT later as you move into senior leadership.
