Hacking: White Hats, Black Hats and the Myths

Why this matters. Hacking is the most misunderstood word in technology. Every defender needs to understand how attackers think, and every organisation needs ethical hackers to find weaknesses first. This module explains what hacking really is, who does it, how ethical hacking and penetration testing work, what the law says, and which popular beliefs are myths. It follows the style of CompTIA PenTest+ and EC-Council CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker) study material: concepts, ethics and law rather than attack instructions.

You will be able to

  • Explain the difference between white, black and grey hats, and red, blue and purple teams.
  • Describe the stages of a typical attack and the defences that break each one.
  • Outline the phases, types and paperwork of a professional penetration test.
  • Explain why authorisation is the legal line, and how coordinated vulnerability disclosure works.
  • Separate common hacking myths from reality and plan a legal path into ethical hacking.

1. What "hacking" really means

In films, a hacker is a lone genius in a hoodie typing furiously until a screen says "ACCESS GRANTED". Real hacking is slower, more methodical and usually done by teams. The word itself did not start out meaning crime.

  • 1950s to 1960s: at MIT (Massachusetts Institute of Technology), members of a model railway club and early computer users called a clever, playful solution to a technical problem a "hack". A hacker was someone who loved understanding how systems really work.
  • 1970s to 1980s: "phone phreaks" explored the telephone network, and personal computers and modems spread. The media started using "hacker" for people who broke into systems.
  • Today: the word has both meanings. Security professionals often say attacker or threat actor for criminals, and ethical hacker or penetration tester for people paid to find weaknesses before criminals do.

The skills are the same on both sides: understanding networks, operating systems, applications and people well enough to see where they break. What separates a professional from a criminal is permission, intent and what happens to the findings.

A locksmith and a burglar both understand locks. The locksmith works with the owner's permission, explains the weakness and fixes it. The burglar uses the same knowledge to steal. Society needs locksmiths precisely because burglars exist.

2. White, black, grey and the rest of the hats

The "hat" names come from old Western films where heroes wore white hats and villains wore black ones. They describe intent and authorisation, not skill.

LabelWho they areLegal?
White hatEthical hackers and penetration testers who test systems with written permission and report findings to the ownerYes, within the agreed scope
Black hatCriminals who break in for money, data, disruption or revengeNo
Grey hatPeople who probe systems without permission but claim good intentions, sometimes reporting the flaw or asking for a "reward"Usually no: good intentions don't make unauthorised access legal
Red teamAn authorised group that imitates real attackers to test an organisation's detection and responseYes, with authorisation
Blue teamDefenders: the SOC (Security Operations Center), incident responders and engineers (Modules 18 and 21)Yes
Purple teamRed and blue working together so every attack technique tested becomes a better detectionYes
Bug bounty hunterIndependent researchers who test programs that publicly invite testing, under published rules, for rewardsYes, if they follow the program's rules
HacktivistAttackers motivated by political or social causesNo

Exam and real-world rule: the only thing that makes hacking "white hat" is prior written authorisation from someone who has the right to give it. A good motive after the fact does not count.

3. How attacks unfold: the attacker's lifecycle

Defenders study how attacks progress so they can break the chain early. Two models are used everywhere: the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain and MITRE ATT&CK, which catalogues real-world tactics and techniques (Module 21). In plain terms, most intrusions move through these stages.

StageWhat the attacker is trying to doWhat defenders do about it
ReconnaissanceLearn about the target: staff names, email formats, internet-facing systems, leaked passwordsReduce public exposure; monitor for leaked credentials; train staff
Initial accessGet a first foothold, most often through phishing, stolen passwords or an unpatched internet-facing systemPhishing-resistant MFA (Multifactor Authentication), patching, email filtering
Execution and persistenceRun their code and make sure it survives a rebootEDR (Endpoint Detection and Response), application allow-listing, hardening
Privilege escalationTurn a normal user's access into administrator accessLeast privilege, patching, separate admin accounts
Lateral movementMove from the first machine to more valuable onesNetwork segmentation, monitoring of admin logins
Collection and exfiltrationFind and steal valuable dataData loss prevention, egress monitoring, encryption
ImpactEncrypt (ransomware), destroy or disruptOffline backups, incident response plans

Notice that most of the defences are not exotic. Reports from national cyber agencies repeatedly find that the majority of successful attacks begin with the basics: weak or stolen passwords, no MFA, unpatched systems and people tricked by phishing.

If you remember one thing: attackers only need one way in, but they need many steps to reach their goal. Every stage is a chance for defenders to detect and stop them.

4. Ethical hacking and penetration testing

A penetration test (pen test) is an authorised, simulated attack to find and prove weaknesses so they can be fixed. A vulnerability scan is an automated check for known weaknesses. A pen tester goes further, using judgement to chain weaknesses together and show real business impact, while staying inside agreed limits.

The phases of a professional test

  1. Planning and scoping (engagement management): agree goals, which systems are in and out of scope, dates and times, who to call in an emergency, and how sensitive data will be handled. Sign the contract and rules of engagement. The written authorisation is sometimes nicknamed the "get out of jail free letter".
  2. Reconnaissance and enumeration: gather information about in-scope systems, both passively (public sources) and actively (scanning).
  3. Vulnerability discovery and analysis: identify weaknesses and rule out false positives.
  4. Exploitation: carefully prove that selected weaknesses are real, avoiding damage and stopping where the rules say to.
  5. Post-exploitation: show what an attacker could reach from there (for example, sensitive data or admin rights), then clean up anything the test left behind.
  6. Reporting: the most valuable part. An executive summary of risk in business terms, plus technical findings with evidence, severity ratings and clear remediation steps.
  7. Retest: after fixes, confirm the weaknesses are closed.

Types of tests

TypeMeaning
Black box (unknown environment)Testers start with little or no information, like an outside attacker
Grey box (partially known)Some information, such as a normal user account
White box (known environment)Full information: diagrams, source code, credentials. Finds the most in the time available
By targetNetwork (external and internal), web application, mobile, cloud, wireless, social engineering, physical

Common methodologies include NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) SP (Special Publication) 800-115, the Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES), the OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Web Security Testing Guide and OSSTMM. Payment card rules (PCI (Payment Card Industry) DSS (Data Security Standard)) require regular penetration tests for in-scope environments, and many regulators and customers expect them.

If something outside the scope looks vulnerable during a test, the professional move is to stop and tell the client, not to "just take a quick look". Testing outside scope can be illegal even during a paid engagement.

5. Hacking and the law

Almost every country has laws against accessing computers without permission. The details differ, but the core idea is the same: authorisation is everything.

LawWhereKey point
Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), 1986United StatesMakes it a crime to access a computer "without authorization" or to "exceed authorized access". A 2021 Supreme Court case (Van Buren v. United States) narrowed what "exceeds authorized access" means.
Computer Misuse Act 1990United KingdomOffences for unauthorised access, access with intent to commit further crimes, and impairing a computer's operation.
Directive 2013/40/EUEuropean UnionRequires member states to criminalise illegal access, system and data interference, and illegal interception.
Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001)International treatyHarmonises cybercrime laws and cooperation between dozens of countries.
Privacy and data lawsMany (GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) and others)Testers who see personal data must protect it; the organisation may have breach-notification duties (Module 32).

Good-faith security research and disclosure

  • In 2022 the US Department of Justice announced it would not charge good-faith security research under the CFAA, but it still defines good faith narrowly and it does not protect against lawsuits or other laws.
  • Coordinated vulnerability disclosure means reporting a flaw privately to the owner and giving them time to fix it before publishing. Standards such as ISO/IEC (ISO: International Organization for Standardization; IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 29147 describe how organisations should receive reports.
  • Many organisations publish a vulnerability disclosure policy or a security.txt file saying how to report issues, and some run bug bounty programs with "safe harbour" terms for researchers who follow the rules.
This is general information, not legal advice. Laws vary by country and change. Before testing anything you don't own, get written permission from someone with the authority to give it, and keep it.

6. Myths and reality

MythReality
"Hackers can break into anything in seconds."Real intrusions take days, weeks or months of patient work. Most succeed through simple failures: reused passwords, no MFA, unpatched systems.
"Hacking is mostly clever code."A large share of breaches involve the human element: phishing, social engineering, mistakes and misuse.
"I'm not important enough to be hacked."Most attacks are automated and opportunistic. Bots scan the whole internet for weak spots and don't care who you are.
"Macs, phones and Linux can't be hacked."Every system has vulnerabilities. Some are targeted less often, which is not the same as being safe.
"Antivirus or a firewall makes me secure."They help, but security is layered: updates, MFA, backups, least privilege and awareness all matter.
"Incognito mode or a VPN (Virtual Private Network) makes me anonymous."Incognito only stops your own device saving history. A VPN moves trust to the VPN provider; it doesn't make you invisible.
"Hackers are hooded loners."Criminal groups run like businesses, with developers, affiliates, negotiators and help desks. Ethical hackers are ordinary professionals with jobs, contracts and reports.
"Ethical hacking is just breaking in."Most of a pen tester's time goes into planning, careful testing, documentation and writing a clear report.
"If I find a flaw, I can prove it by exploiting it."Without permission that is a crime in most countries. Report it through the organisation's disclosure process instead.
"The dark web is where all hacking happens."Some criminal markets live there, but most attacks are run from ordinary rented servers and stolen cloud accounts.

7. Famous cases and what they teach

CaseWhat happenedLesson
Morris worm (1988)A graduate student's experiment spread far faster than intended and slowed a large share of the early internet. He became the first person convicted under the CFAA.Connected systems fail together; intent doesn't undo harm.
Kevin Mitnick (1990s)One of the most-wanted hackers of his era, famous mainly for social engineering. After prison he became a security consultant and author.People are often the easiest way in.
WannaCry (2017)Ransomware spread worldwide through an unpatched Windows flaw, hitting hospitals and factories. A researcher slowed it by registering a "kill switch" domain.Patching matters; one researcher can make a difference.
SolarWinds (2020)Attackers inserted malicious code into a trusted software update used by thousands of organisations, including government agencies.Supply chain attacks bypass perimeter defences.
Colonial Pipeline (2021)Ransomware led a major US fuel pipeline to shut down operations for several days. Public reporting tied the entry to a VPN account password with no MFA.MFA on remote access is essential.
MOVEit (2023)A criminal group exploited a previously unknown flaw in a file-transfer product to steal data from many organisations at once.Internet-facing software needs rapid patching and monitoring.
Notice the pattern: almost every case is a known category (phishing, unpatched software, missing MFA, trusted software abused). Learning the basics in Modules 16 to 18 really does stop most attacks.

8. Becoming an ethical hacker

Offensive security is a popular goal, but it builds on everything else. Testers who don't understand networks, operating systems and how organisations work produce weak reports.

  1. Foundations first: A+, Network+ and Security+ level knowledge (Modules 4 to 14 and 16 to 18), plus comfort with Linux, Windows and basic scripting.
  2. Learn defence too: knowing how attacks are detected (Modules 18 and 21) makes you a better tester and a better report writer.
  3. Practise legally: build a home lab with intentionally vulnerable virtual machines, use capture-the-flag events and training platforms that grant permission, and join bug bounty programs only after reading their rules carefully.
  4. Certifications:
CertificationBodyStyle
PenTest+ (PT0-003)CompTIAVendor-neutral, multiple choice and performance-based; good first offensive cert
CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker)EC-CouncilBroad knowledge exam, well known to HR (Human Resources) and government; an optional practical exam exists
OSCP (OffSec Certified Professional)OffSecHands-on exam compromising lab machines and writing a report; highly respected
GPEN, GWAPTGIAC (Global Information Assurance Certification)Penetration testing and web app testing, tied to SANS (SysAdmin, Audit, Network and Security Institute) training
Never practise on your employer's, school's or anyone else's systems without written permission, even if you think it is harmless. People have lost jobs and faced prosecution for "just checking".
