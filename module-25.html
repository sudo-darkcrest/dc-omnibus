Checking the System: Information Systems Auditing

Why this matters. Every control in this Omnibus, from a firewall rule to a backup schedule, only counts if someone can prove it works. IT (Information Technology) auditors give boards, regulators and customers that independent assurance. ISACA (Information Systems Audit and Control Association)'s Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) is the leading credential for it and is valued in audit firms, banks, government and any organization under SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act), PCI (Payment Card Industry) DSS (Data Security Standard) or ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 27001. This module teaches the auditor's mindset (independence, evidence, risk, reporting) across all five CISA domains and ends with a 75-question practice exam.

You will be able to

  • Plan and run a risk-based IS audit under ISACA standards, collect evidence, sample, use CAATs (Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques), report and follow up.
  • Evaluate IT governance, strategy, organizational structure, segregation of duties, risk, vendors and performance.
  • Audit system acquisition and development: business cases, SDLC (System Development Life Cycle), Agile and DevOps controls, testing, changeover and post-implementation review.
  • Audit IT operations and resilience: scheduling, change, configuration, patching, incidents, assets, databases, capacity, BIA (Business Impact Analysis), backup, BCP (Business Continuity Plan) and DRP (Disaster Recovery Plan).
  • Audit protection of information assets: frameworks, privacy, physical controls, IAM (Identity and Access Management), network, data, encryption, cloud and monitoring.

Think like an auditor

  • Independence: you evaluate and recommend; you don't design, operate or fix the controls you audit.
  • Evidence: conclusions must rest on sufficient, reliable, relevant evidence. Evidence you obtain directly from the system beats what someone tells you.
  • Risk-based: focus effort where risk to the business is highest.
  • Report: the auditor's output is a report to management and the audit committee, with findings and recommendations. Management owns remediation.
Answers where the auditor "fixes the problem", "rolls back the change" or "disables the account." The auditor reports it, with evidence and a recommendation, to the right level of management.

2. Domain 1: The IS auditing process

  • Standards and ethics: ISACA IS Audit and Assurance Standards (general, performance, reporting), guidelines, tools and techniques, and the Code of Professional Ethics. Also IIA (Institute of Internal Auditors) standards for internal audit generally.
  • Audit charter: approved by the board/audit committee; defines authority, scope, responsibility and reporting line (functionally to the audit committee).
  • Risk-based planning: understand the business, assess inherent risk, control risk and detection risk (audit risk = IR × CR × DR), then set scope and objectives.
  • Types of audit: compliance, financial, operational, integrated, IS, forensic, specialized (e.g., third-party/SOC), administrative; internal vs external.
  • Controls: preventive, detective, corrective (also deterrent, compensating); general (ITGC, Information Technology General Controls: access, change, operations, backup) vs application (input, processing, output).
  • Testing: compliance tests check controls operate; substantive tests check the data. Weak controls mean more substantive testing.
  • Evidence: inquiry (weakest), observation, inspection, reperformance, analytics, confirmation. Independent and system-generated is strongest.
  • Sampling: statistical (random, systematic, stratified; projectable) vs judgmental. Attribute sampling for rates (compliance), variable sampling for amounts (substantive). Higher confidence or lower tolerable error means a larger sample. Try Lab 2.
  • CAATs and analytics: generalized audit software, scripts, test data, integrated test facility, embedded audit modules, continuous auditing; test 100% of a population where possible.
  • Reporting: findings with criteria, condition, cause, effect and recommendation; management responses and action dates; communicated to the right level.
  • Follow-up: verify that agreed actions were completed; escalate if risk is accepted without authority.

3. Domain 2: Governance and management of IT

  • IT governance: strategy aligned with business strategy, steering committees, frameworks (COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies) 2019; see Module 23), value delivery and benefits realization.
  • Policies, standards and procedures: approved by senior management, communicated, reviewed regularly.
  • Organizational structure: CIO (Chief Information Officer), CISO (Chief Information Security Officer) and audit reporting lines; security independent of operations.
  • Segregation of duties: separate authorization, recording, custody and reconciliation; in IT, separate development, testing, production operations and security administration. Where SoD isn't possible, use compensating controls (supervisory review, logging). Try Lab 3.
  • Enterprise architecture (TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework), Zachman), IT risk management, maturity models, resource and vendor management (contracts, SLAs (Service Level Agreement), right to audit, exit plans), quality management (ISO 9001) and performance monitoring (balanced scorecard, KPIs (Key Performance Indicator)).

4. Domain 3: Acquisition, development and implementation

  • Project governance: sponsor, steering committee, project manager; scope, time, cost, quality; earned value; stage gates.
  • Business case and feasibility: technical, economic (ROI (Return on Investment), NPV (Net Present Value), payback), operational, legal and schedule feasibility.
  • SDLC models: waterfall (sequential, heavy documentation), Agile (iterative sprints, backlog, definition of done), DevOps / DevSecOps (CI/CD (Continuous Integration / Continuous Delivery) with automated tests and security gates), prototyping, RAD (Rapid Application Development).
  • Development controls: requirements sign-off, secure coding, code review, separate dev/test/prod environments, version control, migration by someone other than the developer (or automated pipeline with approvals).
  • Testing: unit → integration → system (functional, performance, security, regression) → UAT (User Acceptance Testing) by business users; test data should not be live sensitive data unless masked.
  • Migration and changeover: data conversion with reconciliation (record counts, control totals); parallel, pilot, phased or direct cutover with a fallback plan. Try Lab 4.
  • Post-implementation review: benefits vs the business case, lessons learned, usually some months after go-live.

A backup job's "success" log only proves the job ran. Restore tests prove recovery works. On the exam, the evidence that tests the outcome usually beats the evidence that shows activity.

6. Domain 5: Protection of information assets

  • Frameworks: ISO 27001/27002, NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) CSF (Cybersecurity Framework), CIS (Center for Internet Security; Module 23). Privacy principles: lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, accountability; privacy by design; DPIAs (Data Protection Impact Assessment).
  • Physical and environmental: perimeter, mantraps, badges, CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television), visitor logs; fire suppression, temperature and humidity, water detection, UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply; Module 20).
  • Identity and access management: joiner-mover-leaver, least privilege, role-based access, MFA (Multifactor Authentication), privileged access management, periodic access reviews by owners.
  • Network and endpoint: segmentation, firewalls, IDS/IPS (IDS: Intrusion Detection System; IPS: Intrusion Prevention System), VPN (Virtual Private Network), wireless (WPA3-Enterprise), EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response), hardening, MDM (Mobile Device Management).
  • Data: classification by owners, DLP (Data Loss Prevention), retention, secure disposal (wipe, degauss, shred, crypto-erase).
  • Encryption and PKI (Public Key Infrastructure): symmetric vs asymmetric, hashing, digital signatures (integrity, authentication, non-repudiation), CA/RA/CRL/OCSP (CA: Certificate Authority; OCSP: Online Certificate Status Protocol), key management.
  • Cloud and virtualization: shared responsibility, SOC 2 reports, right to audit, hypervisor hardening, data location.
  • Monitoring, incident response and evidence: centralized time-synced logs, SIEM (Security Information and Event Management), IR plans, forensics with chain of custody and hashes.

7. An audit from start to finish

Here is how an IT audit of user access management unfolds, so the phases in this module feel concrete.

  1. Planning: the auditor reviews the risk assessment. Access management is high-risk because the company recently had a breach involving an ex-employee's account. Scope: the finance system, last 12 months.
  2. Understanding controls: interviews and documents show three key controls: access is approved by the manager before creation; leavers are disabled within one business day; access is reviewed quarterly.
  3. Testing design: do these controls, if they work, address the risk? Yes.
  4. Testing operating effectiveness: the auditor samples 25 new accounts (checking for approval), 25 leavers from HR (Human Resources) records (checking disable dates) and all four quarterly reviews.
  5. Findings: 3 of 25 leavers were disabled 5 to 12 days late; one quarterly review was never done.
  6. Reporting: each finding lists the condition (what was found), criteria (what should happen), cause, effect (risk) and a recommendation. Management responds with an action plan and dates.
  7. Follow-up: months later, the auditor checks whether the actions were completed and work.
Auditors recommend; they don't implement fixes themselves. Doing so would damage their independence, because they would later be auditing their own work.

8. What makes good audit evidence?

CISA questions often ask which evidence is the most reliable. Use these rules.

More reliableLess reliable
Evidence from an independent outside source (bank confirmation)Evidence produced by the person being audited
Evidence the auditor obtains directly (re-performing a calculation, observing a process)Evidence obtained indirectly (being told)
System-generated logs with integrity controlsSpreadsheets someone typed by hand
DocumentsVerbal statements in interviews
Original documentsPhotocopies or screenshots that could be edited

Evidence should be sufficient (enough of it), appropriate (relevant and reliable) and documented in working papers so another auditor could reach the same conclusion.

Interviews are useful for understanding a process, but an auditor should always confirm what they are told with documents, system data or observation.
