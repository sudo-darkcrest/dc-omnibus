Running IT as a Service: ITIL 4

Version note (November 2026). PeopleCert launched ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library; Version 5) and its new ITIL Foundation (Version 5) exam in February 2026. ITIL 4 Foundation is still offered alongside it during a transition period; PeopleCert has said the two run in parallel for at least 12 months, and some training providers quote an ITIL 4 sunset at the end of 2027 (verify on peoplecert.org before you book). What changed in Version 5, according to published summaries (verify):
  • ITIL is reframed from "IT (Information Technology) service management" to digital product and service management.
  • The six-activity service value chain is replaced by an eight-activity Product and Service Lifecycle Model: Discover, Design, Build, Transition, Deliver, Support, Operate, Evolve.
  • The seven guiding principles and the four dimensions are kept, and all 34 practices remain (practice groups are reorganized).
  • New guidance on AI (Artificial Intelligence) (governance, ethics, risk), experience (user, customer, employee) and observability.
  • Foundation exam format looks the same: 40 multiple-choice questions, 60 minutes, 65% to pass. ITIL 5 certificates carry a three-year renewal; existing ITIL 4 Foundation holders do not need to retake, and an optional short "Foundation Bridge" exam exists.
This module teaches the ITIL 4 Foundation syllabus, because most of it (concepts, principles, dimensions, practices) carries straight into Version 5. Where Version 5 differs, the lessons say so.

Why this matters. Technology only creates value when people can rely on it. ITIL is the most widely used framework for running IT as a set of services: taking requests, fixing outages, finding root causes, making safe changes and improving over time. Service desks, operations teams, data centers and cloud providers all use its language, and many job ads ask for ITIL 4 Foundation. It also links directly to Security+ change management and incident response, and to CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor)'s IT operations domain.

You will be able to

  • Define service, product, service offering, value, outcomes, costs, risks, utility and warranty, and the roles of customer, user and sponsor.
  • Explain the four dimensions of service management and the PESTLE (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental) factors that affect them.
  • Describe the service value system and the six activities of the service value chain, and combine them into value streams.
  • Apply the seven guiding principles to real situations.
  • Recall the purpose of 15 ITIL practices and explain the seven practices examined in detail.
  • Relate ITIL to DevOps, Agile, Lean and ISO/IEC (ISO: International Organization for Standardization; IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 20000, and know what changed in ITIL (Version 5).

1. Key concepts of service management

A restaurant is a service. You want the outcome "a nice dinner without cooking." The restaurant removes costs (buying ingredients, washing up) and risks (burning the food) but imposes others (the bill, the wait). The menu is the service offering. The food is the utility; the clean kitchen, short wait and safe food are the warranty.

Roles and relationships

A service relationship is a cooperation between a provider and a consumer. It includes service provision (the provider managing resources and delivering), service consumption (the consumer using and paying) and service relationship management (joint activities to keep co-creating value). Organizations are often both providers and consumers in a chain of relationships.

"The customer and the user are always different people." Not true: they are roles, and one person can be customer, user and sponsor at once.

2. The four dimensions of service management

DimensionCoversAsk yourself
Organizations and peopleStructure, roles, culture, skills, staffing, leadership, communicationDo people know their roles, and does the culture support trust and collaboration?
Information and technologyThe information and knowledge needed, and the technology (tools, AI, cloud, databases) that supports servicesWhat data does this service manage, and which tools do we need?
Partners and suppliersRelationships with other organizations involved in design, delivery and support; sourcing strategyWhat do we buy, outsource or integrate, and why?
Value streams and processesHow parts of the organization work together: workflows, activities, controlsWhat are the steps, and where is the waste?

PESTLE factors constrain and influence all four dimensions: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental. A new privacy law (legal) can change the information you can store, the suppliers you can use and the processes you follow.

3. The service value system (SVS)

The SVS shows how all of an organization's components and activities work together to turn opportunity and demand into value. Its five components are the guiding principles, governance, the service value chain, practices and continual improvement. The SVS aims to break down silos and make the organization flexible. Explore it in Lab 1.

  • Opportunity: options to add value for stakeholders. Demand: the need or desire for products and services.
  • Governance: the means by which an organization is directed and controlled. The governing body evaluates, directs and monitors.
  • Practices: sets of organizational resources for performing work. ITIL 4 has 34 practices in three groups: general management (14), service management (17) and technical management (3).
  • Continual improvement happens at every level, from strategy down to individual tasks.

4. The seven guiding principles

A guiding principle is a recommendation that guides an organization in all circumstances. Consider all seven together and decide which matter most in each situation; they interact.

PrincipleIn one lineExample
Focus on valueEverything should link to value for someone; know your consumerBefore adding a new report, ask which decision it helps and who reads it
Start where you areDon't start from scratch; observe the current state directly and reuse what worksWalk the service desk floor before redesigning its process
Progress iteratively with feedbackSmall, manageable steps with feedback loopsRoll out self-service to one department, gather feedback, then expand
Collaborate and promote visibilityWork across boundaries; make work and progress visibleA shared Kanban board shows every team where requests are stuck
Think and work holisticallyNo service or part stands alone; see the whole systemCheck how a database change affects monitoring, support and suppliers
Keep it simple and practicalUse the minimum steps; remove what adds no value; outcome-based thinkingDrop an approval step that nobody ever rejects
Optimize and automateOptimize first, then automate; use technology to free people for valuable workSimplify the onboarding workflow, then automate account creation
In ITIL (Version 5) the seven principles keep the same names (verify).

Value chain activities use different combinations of practices. Incident management, for example, contributes to engage, deliver and support, improve, and more. In ITIL (Version 5) the value chain is replaced by the eight-activity Product and Service Lifecycle Model (Discover, Design, Build, Transition, Deliver, Support, Operate, Evolve; verify).

6. The continual improvement model

  1. What is the vision? Understand the high-level business direction.
  2. Where are we now? Objective baseline assessment.
  3. Where do we want to be? Measurable targets (gap analysis).
  4. How do we get there? A plan, often as iterations.
  5. Take action. Carry out the plan.
  6. Did we get there? Check results against targets.
  7. How do we keep the momentum going? Embed the change, celebrate success, start the next loop.

Ideas are recorded and prioritized in a continual improvement register (CIR). Continual improvement is everyone's responsibility.

Incident management

  • An incident is an unplanned interruption to a service or a reduction in its quality. Restore service fast; finding the cause is problem management's job.
  • Log every incident, prioritize by agreed classification (impact × urgency), keep users informed and update the record with symptoms, impact, CIs and actions.
  • Major incidents get a separate procedure; security incidents may too. Some incidents are resolved by users (self-help), the service desk, support teams, suppliers or temporary teams. Swarming brings experts together.

Problem management

  • A problem is a cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents. A known error is a problem that has been analyzed but not resolved. A workaround reduces impact until a permanent fix.
  • Three phases: problem identification (trend analysis, recurring incidents, information from suppliers and developers), problem control (analysis using all four dimensions, documenting workarounds) and error control (managing known errors, assessing permanent solutions).
  • Permanent fixes usually go through change enablement.

Change enablement

  • A change is adding, modifying or removing anything that could directly or indirectly affect services.
  • Standard: low risk, pre-authorized, well understood, often a service request. Normal: scheduled, assessed and authorized by the right change authority. Emergency: implemented as soon as possible (e.g., a critical patch), possibly with a separate authority and less documentation, but still assessed.
  • ITIL 4 decentralizes change authority: a CAB (Change Advisory Board) is just one option; peer review in a DevOps pipeline can authorize low-risk changes. The change schedule helps plan, communicate and avoid conflicts.

Service request management

  • A service request is a user request that starts an agreed, normal service action: information, access, a laptop, a password reset, feedback or a complaint.
  • Standardize and automate as much as possible; set clear policies on approvals; set realistic, agreed fulfilment times; some requests trigger a standard change.

Service desk

  • The single point of contact for all users. Channels: phone, chat, email, portals, walk-in, social media. Can be centralized, virtual or follow-the-sun.
  • Staff need empathy, communication, customer-service skills, incident analysis and prioritization, and a practical understanding of the business, not only technical depth.

Service level management

  • Sets business-based targets and makes sure delivery is measured against them. An SLA (Service Level Agreement) should relate to a defined service, describe outcomes (not just operational metrics) and be written in clear language.
  • Beware the watermelon SLA: green metrics, red customers. Gather customer engagement, feedback, surveys and business metrics.
  • Related terms: OLA (Operational Level Agreement) (between internal teams) and UC (underpinning contract with an external supplier). These come from earlier ITIL versions and are still common at work.
"Incident management finds the root cause." No: incident management restores service; problem management finds and manages causes. A help desk that "fixes it by rebooting" closes the incident; the problem stays open until the cause is known.

8. How ITIL fits with DevOps, Agile, Lean and ISO/IEC 20000

ApproachHow it relates to ITIL 4
AgileIterative delivery and fast feedback; matches "progress iteratively with feedback" and value streams
DevOpsJoins development and operations with automation (CI/CD); ITIL 4 supports this with decentralized change authority and "optimize and automate"
LeanRemoving waste and improving flow; matches value stream mapping and "keep it simple and practical"
ISO/IEC 20000-1The international standard for a service management system (SMS); organizations can be certified against it. ITIL is guidance, not a certifiable standard for organizations (people get ITIL certificates; organizations get ISO/IEC 20000 certification)

9. ITIL in action: one incident, many practices

ITIL practices make the most sense when you see them work together. Follow one email outage through an organisation that uses ITIL 4.

  1. Monitoring and event management detects that the email service is failing health checks at 10:02 and raises an alert.
  2. Service desk starts receiving calls and links them to one major incident, keeping users updated through the status page.
  3. Incident management aims to restore service as fast as possible. Engineers fail over to the backup server at 10:40. Service is restored; the incident is resolved.
  4. Problem management then asks why it happened. The root cause: a certificate expired because nobody owned its renewal. A known error is recorded with the workaround.
  5. Change enablement approves a change to add automatic certificate renewal and expiry alerts.
  6. Service configuration management updates the configuration record so certificates appear as configuration items with owners.
  7. Service level management reports the 38-minute outage against the agreed availability target.
  8. Continual improvement adds "inventory all certificates" to the improvement register.
Exam favourite: incident management restores service quickly; problem management finds and removes the underlying cause. They are different practices with different goals.

10. Words ITIL uses in a specific way

ITIL Foundation questions often hinge on precise definitions. These are the ones people mix up most.

TermITIL meaning
ServiceA means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes customers want, without the customer managing specific costs and risks
Output vs outcomeAn output is something produced (a report); an outcome is the result for a stakeholder (a better decision)
Utility vs warrantyUtility: what the service does (fit for purpose). Warranty: how well it does it (fit for use: availability, capacity, security, continuity)
IncidentAn unplanned interruption or reduction in quality of a service
ProblemA cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents
Known errorA problem that has been analysed but not yet resolved
ChangeAdding, modifying or removing anything that could directly or indirectly affect services
Service requestA user request that is a normal part of service delivery (a password reset, a new laptop)
A password reset is a service request, not an incident: nothing is broken; the user needs a standard thing done.

Exam trapWhy it is wrong
"Value is delivered by the provider to the consumer."ITIL 4 says value is co-created.
"A standard change needs CAB approval."It is pre-authorized; only the procedure was assessed.
"Release and deployment are the same."Deployment moves components; release makes them available for use.
