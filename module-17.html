Know the Enemy: Threats, Attacks and Vulnerabilities

Why this matters. You can't defend against what you can't recognize. Security+ Domain 2 (Threats, Vulnerabilities and Mitigations) is about 22% of the exam, and every later topic (architecture, operations, incident response) assumes you can name the attack and the control that stops it. Everything here is explained conceptually and defensively: how the attack works, how to prevent it, how to detect it and how to respond. There is no working exploit code anywhere on this page, and the SQL (Structured Query Language) injection lab is a simulation with no real database.

You will be able to

  • Name threat actors, their motives and resources, and the vectors they use.
  • Recognize social engineering and phishing red flags in real-looking messages.
  • Tell malware types and application, network, password and crypto attacks apart, and pick the control for each.
  • Describe vulnerability types, indicators of compromise, MITRE ATT&CK and the Cyber Kill Chain.
  • Score a vulnerability with CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) and run a vulnerability management cycle, including pen test rules of engagement.

Cert map

1. Threat actors, vectors and attack surface

A threat actor is whoever might attack you. Exam questions describe an attacker and ask you to name them, so focus on resources/sophistication, internal vs external, and motive.

ActorResources and skillTypical motiveClue in a question
Nation-stateVery high; custom tools, zero-days, patience (APT = advanced persistent threat)Espionage, disruption, war, political"Long-term undetected access to a government contractor"
Organized crimeHigh; professional, ransomware-as-a-serviceFinancial gain"Encrypted files and demanded cryptocurrency"
HacktivistLow to mediumIdeology, philosophical/political beliefs"Defaced the website with a protest message", DDoS for a cause
Insider threatVaries, but has legitimate access and knowledgeRevenge, financial gain, carelessness"A disgruntled employee copied the customer list"
Unskilled attacker (script kiddie)Low; runs others' toolsThrill, notoriety, disruption"Used a downloaded tool without understanding it"
Shadow IT (Information Technology)Internal, well-meaningConvenience (gets work done around IT)"A team signed up for an unapproved cloud file service"

Motives the exam lists: data exfiltration, espionage, service disruption, blackmail, financial gain, philosophical/political beliefs, ethical (white hat), revenge, disruption/chaos, war.

Threat vectors and attack surface

The attack surface is every place an attacker could try to get in; a threat vector is the path they use. Vectors include: messages (email, SMS (Short Message Service), instant messaging), image-based and file-based (malicious attachments), voice calls, removable devices (USB (Universal Serial Bus) drops), vulnerable software (client-based vs agentless), unsupported systems and applications, unsecure networks (wireless, wired, Bluetooth), open service ports, default credentials, and the supply chain (managed service providers, vendors, suppliers).

Defender's view: reduce the attack surface first (close ports, remove unused software, change defaults, retire unsupported systems), then harden what must stay exposed.

2. Social engineering

Social engineering attacks the person, not the computer. It works through authority, urgency, scarcity, familiarity/liking, social proof, intimidation and trust.

AttackHow it worksDefense
PhishingMass fraudulent email luring clicks, credentials or attachmentsAwareness training, email filtering, SPF/DKIM/DMARC (SPF: Sender Policy Framework; DMARC: Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance), MFA (Multifactor Authentication), reporting button
Spear phishingPhishing tailored to a specific person or group using researched detailsTraining with realistic simulations, verify through a second channel
WhalingSpear phishing aimed at executivesExecutive training, payment approval controls
Vishing / smishingVoice-call / SMS phishing (often fake bank, IT support or delivery)Call back on a known number; never give codes over the phone
PretextingAn invented scenario ("I'm from the auditor's office") to justify the requestIdentity verification procedures
Business email compromise (BEC)Compromised or spoofed executive/vendor mailbox requests wire transfers or changes to bank detailsOut-of-band verification for any payment change, dual approval
Watering holeCompromise a website the target group visitsPatching, browser isolation, web filtering, EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response)
Typosquatting / brand impersonationLook-alike domains (examp1e.com) or fake brand pagesRegister look-alikes, DNS (Domain Name System) filtering, check the real domain
ImpersonationPretending to be someone trusted (IT, boss, vendor), in person or remotelyVerification, badges, "challenge strangers" culture
Misinformation / disinformationFalse content, accidental or deliberate, to influence peopleMedia literacy, official channels
a phishing email is a stranger in a delivery uniform at your door. The uniform (logo, display name) is easy to fake; the van's licence plate (the real sender domain and the real link destination) is what you check.

Red flags to train your eye

  • Sender: display name doesn't match the address; look-alike domain (ha1cyondata.com, company-corp.com); free mail for a "CEO".
  • Links: displayed text differs from the real URL (Uniform Resource Locator); the real domain is the part just before the first single slash (login.example.com.evil.ru belongs to evil.ru).
  • Urgency or secrecy: "within 2 hours", "keep this confidential", "I'm in a meeting, just do it".
  • Attachments: unexpected, double extensions (invoice.pdf.exe), macro-enabled Office files, password-protected ZIPs.
  • Requests: gift cards, wire transfers, bank detail changes, MFA codes, password resets.

4. Application attacks

AttackConcept (what goes wrong)Prevent / detect
SQL injection (SQLi)User input is pasted into a database query, so input can change the query's meaningParameterized queries (prepared statements), input validation, least-privilege DB accounts, WAF (Web Application Firewall)
Cross-site scripting (XSS)The site echoes attacker-supplied script into pages other users view (stored, reflected, DOM-based (Document Object Model))Output encoding, input validation, Content Security Policy, HttpOnly cookies
Cross-site request forgery (CSRF)A malicious page makes the victim's logged-in browser send an unwanted request to another siteAnti-CSRF tokens, SameSite cookies, re-authentication for sensitive actions
Server-side request forgery (SSRF)The server is tricked into fetching internal URLs (e.g., cloud metadata) for the attackerAllow-list outbound destinations, block metadata endpoints, IMDSv2
Buffer overflowWriting more data than a memory buffer holds overwrites adjacent memory, possibly redirecting executionMemory-safe languages, bounds checking, ASLR (Address Space Layout Randomization), DEP/NX (DEP: Data Execution Prevention), stack canaries
Race condition (TOCTOU)Time-of-check to time-of-use gap: state changes between checking and actingAtomic operations, locking, careful design
Directory traversalPaths like ../../ escape the intended folder to read other filesCanonicalize paths, allow-lists, run with least privilege
Privilege escalationGaining higher rights: vertical (user → admin) or horizontal (user A → user B's data)Patching, least privilege, PAM (Privileged Access Management), monitoring for new admin accounts
ReplayCapturing valid data (token, session, auth exchange) and resending itTimestamps, nonces, session expiry, TLS (Transport Layer Security)
Injection (general)Any input interpreted as commands: OS command, LDAP (Lightweight Directory Access Protocol), XML (Extensible Markup Language)Never build commands from raw input; validate and use safe APIs (Application Programming Interface)
The single idea behind injection: the program mixes code and data in one string. A parameterized query sends them separately, so data can never become code. Lab 2 shows this visually.

6. Password and cryptographic attacks

Password attacks

  • Brute force: try every combination. Online is limited by lockouts; offline (against stolen hashes) is limited only by hardware.
  • Dictionary: try likely words and leaked passwords with variations.
  • Password spraying: a few common passwords against many accounts, staying under lockout thresholds. Clue: many accounts, one failure each.
  • Credential stuffing: username/password pairs leaked from another site.
  • Rainbow tables: precomputed hash lookups; defeated by salting.

Defense: MFA, salted and stretched hashes (bcrypt, Argon2), smart lockout, breached-password checks, passkeys.

Cryptographic attacks

  • Downgrade: force a weaker protocol or cipher (e.g., old SSL/TLS (SSL: Secure Sockets Layer)). Defense: disable legacy versions, TLS 1.3, HSTS.
  • Collision: two different inputs produce the same hash (MD5 (Message Digest 5), SHA-1 (Secure Hash Algorithm) are broken). Defense: SHA-256 or better.
  • Birthday attack: collisions are found far faster than expected (about the square root of the output space), because of the birthday paradox. Defense: longer hash outputs.
  • Side-channel: timing, power or emissions leak keys. Defense: constant-time code, hardware protections.
Birthday paradox: with only 23 people in a room there's a 50% chance two share a birthday. A 128-bit hash gives about 264 work for a collision, not 2128.

8. Indicators of compromise, ATT&CK and the Kill Chain

Indicators of compromise (IoCs) are evidence that an attack happened or is happening. Security+ lists: account lockouts, concurrent session usage, blocked content, impossible travel (logins from two distant places minutes apart), resource consumption, resource inaccessibility, out-of-cycle logging, published/documented (your data shows up on a leak site), and missing logs. Technical IoCs include known-bad IP addresses, domains, file hashes and registry keys.

Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (7 stages)

  1. Reconnaissance: research the target
  2. Weaponization: build the payload
  3. Delivery: send it (email, web, USB)
  4. Exploitation: trigger the vulnerability
  5. Installation: install persistence
  6. Command and control (C2): remote control channel
  7. Actions on objectives: steal, encrypt, destroy

Break any link and the attack fails, which is the point of defense in depth.

MITRE ATT&CK

A public knowledge base of real adversary behavior, organized as tactics (the "why", e.g., Initial Access, Persistence, Lateral Movement, Exfiltration, Impact) and techniques (the "how", e.g., T1566 Phishing). Defenders map detections to techniques to find gaps.

Also know the Diamond Model (adversary, capability, infrastructure, victim) at a recognition level.

ATT&CK Enterprise has 14 tactics: Reconnaissance, Resource Development, Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Defense Evasion, Credential Access, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Collection, Command and Control, Exfiltration, Impact.

9. Vulnerability management

  • Identify: vulnerability scans (credentialed scans see more than non-credentialed; agent vs agentless), application security testing (SAST static, DAST dynamic), package monitoring, threat feeds (OSINT (Open-Source Intelligence), ISACs (Information Sharing and Analysis Center), dark web), penetration tests, bug bounties.
  • Analyze: confirm results (watch for false positives, and worse, false negatives), prioritize using CVSS, the CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) identifier, exposure, environmental factors, asset value and whether it's actively exploited (CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor) KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) catalog).
  • Respond: patch, insurance, segmentation, compensating controls, or formal exceptions/exemptions with risk acceptance.
  • Validate: rescan, audit, verify. Report: metrics to management.

10. Anatomy of a ransomware attack

Individual attack types make more sense when you see how they chain together. This is a realistic, simplified timeline of a ransomware incident at a fictional manufacturer.

DayAttacker actionWhere it could have been stopped
1A phishing email with a fake invoice tricks an accounts clerk into entering her password on a look-alike login pageSecurity awareness training; phishing-resistant MFA
1The attacker logs in to webmail and the VPN with the stolen password; the VPN has no MFAMFA on every remote access path
3They run tools to map the network and find an unpatched file serverPatch management; EDR alerts on scanning
5They steal an administrator's credentials from memory and move to the domain controllersLeast privilege, separate admin accounts, credential protection
8They copy 200 GB (Gigabyte) of design files to a cloud storage siteData loss prevention; alerts on unusual outbound traffic
9They delete backups they can reach, then encrypt hundreds of servers at 2 AM on a SaturdayOffline or immutable backups; 24/7 monitoring
9A ransom note demands payment for the decryption key and to stop the stolen data being publishedAn incident response plan that has been rehearsed (Module 18)

This "double extortion" model (encrypt and threaten to leak) is now common. Notice that no single control would have stopped everything, but several would have stopped it early. That is defense in depth.

Read real incident reports from national cyber agencies. They describe attacker steps in plain language and map them to MITRE ATT&CK techniques (Module 21).

11. Spotting social engineering: the red flags

Technology cannot stop every scam, so people are the last line of defense. Nearly every social engineering attempt uses at least one of these pressure tactics.

  • Urgency: "Your account closes in one hour."
  • Authority: "This is the CEO; I need this done now." or "This is IT support."
  • Fear: "We detected illegal activity on your computer."
  • Greed or curiosity: "You've won a prize" or "Salary review attached."
  • Unusual channel or request: gift cards, payment to a new bank account, a password or MFA code over the phone.
  • Small mismatches: a sender address one letter off, a link whose real address doesn't match the text.
No real IT department will ask for your password or an MFA code. If someone does, it is an attack, however convincing they sound. Verify by calling back on a number you already know.

Organisations add process controls on top of awareness: call-back verification for payment changes, two-person approval for large transfers, and a simple, blame-free way to report suspicious messages.
