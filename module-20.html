The Big Security Certs: CISSP and CCSP

Why this matters. The CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional) is the certification most often requested for senior security roles, and the CCSP (Certified Cloud Security Professional) is its cloud specialist sibling. Both appear on top-paying lists. You need years of experience to hold them, but knowing what they cover helps you plan a career and fills in the management side of security that Security+ only touches.

You will be able to

  • Explain the CISSP and CCSP experience rules, exam formats and the ISC2 (International Information System Security Certification Consortium) code of ethics.
  • Summarize the eight CISSP domains and calculate SLE (Single Loss Expectancy) and ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy).
  • Compare security models, access control models and SOC (System and Organization Controls) report types.
  • Describe recovery site types, evidence handling and secure development practices.
  • Explain the CCSP domains, the cloud data lifecycle and crypto-shredding, and answer questions from a manager's viewpoint.

Cert map

1. CISSP and CCSP: what they are and who can get them

ISC2 is a non-profit membership body for security professionals. Its CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional) is probably the best-known senior security certification, and its CCSP (Certified Cloud Security Professional) is the cloud-focused sibling. Both appear on the top-paying lists because they require years of verified experience.

You can sit either exam without the experience. If you pass, you become an Associate of ISC2 and have six years to gain the experience. After passing, another certified member must endorse your application.

New to security? ISC2's entry-level CC (Certified in Cybersecurity) and CompTIA Security+ (Modules 16 to 18) are the usual first steps. CISSP comes later.

The ISC2 Code of Ethics

Every member agrees to four canons, in priority order: protect society, the common good and the infrastructure; act honourably, honestly, justly, responsibly and legally; provide diligent and competent service to principals; and advance and protect the profession. When canons conflict, the earlier one wins.

Domain 1: Security and Risk Management

  • CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) triad plus authenticity and non-repudiation (Module 16). Due care is doing the right thing; due diligence is checking that it is being done.
  • Risk = likelihood × impact. Quantitative risk uses money: SLE (single loss expectancy) = asset value × exposure factor; ALE (annualized loss expectancy) = SLE × ARO (annual rate of occurrence). Spend no more on a control than the ALE it removes.
  • Risk responses: avoid, mitigate (reduce), transfer (insurance, contracts) or accept. Never ignore.
  • Governance: policies (mandatory, high level), standards (mandatory specifics), procedures (step by step), guidelines (recommended).
  • Business continuity: business impact analysis sets RTO (recovery time objective), RPO (recovery point objective) and MTD (maximum tolerable downtime).
  • Laws and privacy (Module 33), intellectual property, supply chain risk and security awareness training.

Domain 2: Asset Security

  • Classification: label data by sensitivity (for example public, internal, confidential, restricted; or government levels).
  • Roles: the data owner (a senior business person) decides classification and access; the custodian (often IT) implements protection; users follow the rules; under privacy law a controller decides why data is processed and a processor processes it on their behalf.
  • Data states: at rest, in transit, in use. Protect each differently (encryption, TLS (Transport Layer Security), access controls and memory protection).
  • Retention and destruction: keep data only as long as needed. Sanitization methods: clearing (overwrite), purging (degauss or cryptographic erase) and destruction (shred or incinerate).

3. CISSP Domains 3 and 4: architecture and networks

Domain 3: Security Architecture and Engineering

  • Secure design principles: least privilege, defence in depth, secure defaults, fail securely, separation of duties, keep it simple, zero trust and privacy by design.
  • Security models (classic exam favourites):
    • Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality: "no read up, no write down".
    • Biba protects integrity: "no read down, no write up".
    • Clark-Wilson protects integrity through well-formed transactions and separation of duties.
    • Brewer-Nash ("Chinese Wall") prevents conflicts of interest.
  • Cryptography (Module 16): symmetric vs asymmetric, hashing, digital signatures, PKI (public key infrastructure), key management and quantum-safe algorithms.
  • Physical security: site selection, fences, lighting, mantraps, CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television), fire suppression and power (Module 22).
  • Vulnerabilities in hardware, virtualization, cloud, IoT (Internet of Things) and industrial control systems.

Domain 4: Communication and Network Security

  • OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) and TCP/IP (TCP: Transmission Control Protocol; IP: Internet Protocol) models (Module 9), secure protocols (TLS, IPsec (Internet Protocol Security), SSH (Secure Shell)), network segmentation, firewalls, VPNs (Virtual Private Network), wireless security (Module 13) and network attacks.
  • Converged protocols such as voice over IP, Fibre Channel over Ethernet and iSCSI, and software-defined networking (Modules 14 and 15).
Bell-LaPadula is about secrets (confidentiality): you can't read above your clearance. Biba is about bad data (integrity): you can't be polluted by less trustworthy data. The rules are mirror images.

4. CISSP Domains 5 and 6: identity and testing

Domain 5: Identity and Access Management

  • Identification, authentication, authorization and accountability (logging what people do).
  • Access control models: DAC (discretionary: the owner decides), MAC (Mandatory Access Control; mandatory: labels and clearances decide), RBAC (Role-Based Access Control; role-based), ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control; attribute-based: rules using user, resource and environment attributes) and rule-based (firewall-style rules).
  • Federation and SSO (Single Sign-On): SAML (Security Assertion Markup Language), OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect. Kerberos uses tickets from a key distribution center and depends on synchronized clocks.
  • Identity lifecycle: provisioning, access reviews, and timely deprovisioning. Privileged access management for admin accounts.
  • Biometric errors: FRR (false rejection, Type I) and FAR (false acceptance, Type II). The CER (crossover error rate) is where they are equal; lower is better.

Domain 6: Security Assessment and Testing

  • Vulnerability scanning, penetration testing (Module 19), code review, log review, synthetic transactions and breach and attack simulation.
  • Audits: internal, external and third-party. SOC 1 covers controls relevant to financial reporting; SOC 2 covers security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. A Type I report checks design at a point in time; Type II tests operating effectiveness over a period (usually 6 to 12 months).
  • Key performance and risk indicators reported to management.

5. CISSP Domains 7 and 8: operations and software

Domain 7: Security Operations

  • Investigations: administrative, criminal, civil and regulatory. Handle evidence with a chain of custody, collect in order of volatility (memory before disk), and work from verified copies (hashes).
  • Logging and monitoring, SIEM (Security Information and Event Management), threat intelligence, configuration and change management, and patching.
  • Incident response (Module 18): detection, response, mitigation, reporting, recovery, remediation and lessons learned.
  • Recovery sites: hot (ready in minutes or hours), warm (equipment, needs data and setup), cold (space and power only), plus cloud and reciprocal agreements. Test plans by read-through, walk-through (tabletop), simulation, parallel test and full interruption.
  • Personnel safety comes first, always.

Domain 8: Software Development Security

  • Build security into every phase of the SDLC (software development life cycle): requirements, design (threat modelling), coding standards, testing (static and dynamic analysis), deployment and maintenance.
  • Methodologies: waterfall, agile and DevOps / DevSecOps. Maturity models such as the OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) SAMM.
  • Common weaknesses: the OWASP Top 10 (injection, broken access control and others), insecure APIs (Application Programming Interface), and risks from third-party and open-source components (track them with a software bill of materials, SBOM).

  • Cloud data lifecycle: create, store, use, share, archive, destroy.
  • Crypto-shredding: you can't physically shred a provider's disks, so you destroy the encryption keys, making the data unreadable.
  • Customer-managed keys and HSMs (hardware security modules) keep control of keys with the customer.
  • Data sovereignty and residency: laws of the country where data is stored (and the provider's home country) may apply.
  • Assurance: you can't usually audit a hyperscaler yourself, so you rely on their SOC 2 reports, ISO/IEC (ISO: International Organization for Standardization; IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 27001 and 27017/27018 certificates, and the Cloud Security Alliance STAR registry and Cloud Controls Matrix.

7. Think like a manager, study like a marathon

CISSP questions often have several technically correct answers. The best one is usually the answer a risk-aware manager would choose.

  • People's safety first, then the business's goals, then technology.
  • Prefer policy and process answers over "reconfigure the firewall" answers, unless the question is clearly technical.
  • Look for the answer that addresses the root cause, and the step that comes first (for example, get management support before writing policy).
  • Senior management owns risk. Security advises; it doesn't accept risk on the business's behalf.

A realistic plan

  1. Confirm you meet (or are building) the experience requirement.
  2. Study one domain a week with the official guide and practice questions; review weak domains twice.
  3. Take full-length practice exams to build stamina for three hours.
  4. Book the exam when you consistently score well above passing on fresh questions.
Trap. Memorising port numbers won't pass CISSP. Understanding why a control exists, and who decides, will.
