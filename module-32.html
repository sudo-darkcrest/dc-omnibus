Frameworks That Run the Enterprise: Governance, Risk and Compliance

Why this matters. Firewalls and patches only work when someone decides what to protect, how much risk is acceptable, who is accountable and how to prove it to regulators and customers. That is governance, risk and compliance (GRC). This module is the bridge from technical security to the management view of CISM (Certified Information Security Manager; Module 35) and the assurance view of CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor; Module 36), and it covers all of Security+ domain 5.

You will be able to

  • Separate governance from management and assign roles with a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) chart.
  • Explain what COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies), ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 27001/27002, ISO 31000/27005, ISO 22301, ISO 20000, NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) 2.0, SP (Special Publication) 800-53, the RMF (Risk Management Framework), CIS (Center for Internet Security) Controls, SOC (System and Organization Controls) 2, PCI (Payment Card Industry) DSS (Data Security Standard), HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act), SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act) and GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) are each for.
  • Place documents in the policy hierarchy: policies, standards, procedures, guidelines and baselines.
  • Run qualitative and quantitative risk analysis (SLE (Single Loss Expectancy), ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence), ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy)) and decide whether a control is worth its cost.
  • Use risk appetite, tolerance, capacity, KRIs (Key Risk Indicator), KPIs (Key Performance Indicator), KGIs (Key Goal Indicator), risk registers and maturity models to report to leadership.

1. Governance vs management

GovernanceManagement
QuestionAre we doing the right things?Are we doing things right?
WhoBoard of directors, executive leadershipCIO (Chief Information Officer), CISO (Chief Information Security Officer), managers and teams
Activities (COBIT)Evaluate, Direct, MonitorPlan, Build, Run, Monitor
OutputsStrategy, risk appetite, policies, oversightPrograms, projects, controls, operations, metrics
  • Board of directors: accountable to shareholders; approves strategy and risk appetite; often uses an audit committee and a risk committee.
  • Executive management (CEO, CFO, CIO, CISO): turns direction into plans and reports back.
  • Steering committee: senior cross-functional group that prioritizes and funds programs (an IT (Information Technology) or security steering committee).
  • Data roles: owner (accountable business manager, classifies data), custodian/steward (implements controls day to day), controller and processor (privacy law roles: who decides why and how data is processed vs who processes on their behalf).

4. Risk management in depth

Risk = the chance that a threat exploits a vulnerability in an asset, causing impact. Risk is usually expressed as likelihood × impact.

Qualitative analysis

Ratings such as low/medium/high or 1–5 scales for likelihood and impact, plotted on a heat map. Fast and easy to communicate, but subjective.

Quantitative analysis

Money and numbers: SLE = AV (Asset Value) × EF (Exposure Factor), ALE = SLE × ARO. Supports cost-benefit decisions, but needs reliable data.

A control is worth it when (ALE before − ALE after) − annual cost of the control > 0. Annual cost includes licences, staff time and maintenance. Try it in Lab 2.
TermMeaning
Risk appetiteBroad amount and type of risk the organization is willing to pursue (set by the board): "We have a low appetite for regulatory risk."
Risk toleranceAcceptable variation for a specific objective: "No more than 4 hours of downtime per quarter for the store."
Risk capacityThe maximum loss the organization could absorb before failing
Inherent / residual riskBefore controls / after controls. Residual must sit within appetite or be formally accepted
TreatmentMitigate (reduce), transfer/share (insurance, outsourcing), avoid (stop the activity), accept (document and monitor)
Risk registerThe list of risks: ID (Identification), description, owner, likelihood, impact, inherent and residual rating, treatment, due date, status
Risk ownerThe business manager accountable for the risk and authorized to decide treatment
KRI / KPI / KGIKey risk indicator (leading warning, e.g., % of servers unpatched > 30 days) / key performance indicator (how well a process runs, e.g., mean time to patch) / key goal indicator (lagging: was the goal reached, e.g., zero critical audit findings)

Third-party and supply chain risk

Lifecycle: due diligence (questionnaires, SOC 2 reports, ISO certificates, financial health) → contract (security requirements, right to audit, breach notification time, SLAs (Service Level Agreement), data return and deletion) → onboarding → ongoing monitoring (reassessments, security ratings) → offboarding (revoke access, confirm data destruction). Agreements you may see: MSA (Master Service Agreement), SOW (Statement of Work), SLA, NDA (Non-Disclosure Agreement), MOU/MOA (MOU: Memorandum of Understanding; MOA: Memorandum of Agreement), BPA (Blanket Purchase Agreement). Software supply chain controls include SBOMs (Software Bill of Materials) and vendor patch commitments.

5. Metrics, reporting and maturity

  • Report to the board in business terms: risk trends against appetite, top risks and owners, compliance status, major incidents and their business impact, investment versus risk reduction.
  • Report to operations in technical terms: patch latency, vulnerability counts, alert volumes, MTTD (Mean Time To Detect) and MTTR (Mean Time To Repair).
  • Good metrics are SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.

7. Worked example: a risk register entry

A risk register turns worries into decisions. Here is one entry worked through from start to finish.

  • Risk: Ransomware encrypts the file server and its backups.
  • Asset value: the file server and the business it supports are valued at $400,000.
  • Exposure factor: an attack would destroy or disrupt about 50% of that value. Single loss expectancy = $400,000 × 0.5 = $200,000.
  • Likelihood: estimated once every four years. Annualised rate of occurrence = 0.25.
  • Annualised loss expectancy = $200,000 × 0.25 = $50,000 per year.
  • Treatment options: immutable offline backups and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) costing $15,000 a year would cut the likelihood to once every 20 years (0.05), giving a new ALE of $10,000.
  • Decision: spending $15,000 to reduce expected losses by $40,000 a year is worth it. The risk owner accepts the remaining risk and signs off.
The four treatment options are: mitigate (reduce), transfer (insurance, outsourcing), avoid (stop the activity) and accept. Ignoring a risk is not acceptance; acceptance is a documented decision by the risk owner.

Exam trapWhy it is wrong
"Transferring risk transfers accountability."Insurance or outsourcing shares the financial impact; the organization stays accountable.
"The CISO owns all security risks."Business managers own risks; the CISO advises and reports.
