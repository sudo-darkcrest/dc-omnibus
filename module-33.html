Data Privacy

Why this matters. Privacy laws now cover most people on Earth, and organisations need people who understand both the law and the technology. Three privacy certifications appear on the top-paying list. This module explains what privacy is, how the major laws work, how a privacy program runs, and how engineers build privacy into systems.

You will be able to

  • Compare the CIPP, CIPM and CDPSE certifications.
  • Explain personal data, sensitive data, FIPPs and privacy by design.
  • Describe GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) principles, lawful bases, rights, breach rules and transfer mechanisms.
  • Summarize US sector and state privacy laws and other major regimes.
  • Outline a privacy program and choose privacy engineering techniques such as minimisation, pseudonymisation and anonymisation.

1. Privacy as a career and the three certifications

Security protects data from attackers. Privacy is about whether an organisation should collect, use and share personal data at all, and on what terms. Privacy laws now cover most of the world's population, and three privacy certifications are on the top-paying list.

2. Core privacy concepts

  • Personal data / PII (personally identifiable information): any information about an identified or identifiable person: name, email, IP (Internet Protocol) address, location, device IDs.
  • Sensitive (special category) data: health, biometrics, genetics, religion, sexual orientation, political opinions and similar. Stricter rules apply.
  • Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs) and the OECD Privacy Guidelines: collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation and accountability. Most modern laws build on them.
  • Privacy by design (Ann Cavoukian's seven principles): proactive not reactive; privacy as the default; embedded into design; full functionality (positive-sum); end-to-end security; visibility and transparency; respect for user privacy.
  • Notice and choice: tell people what you do and give real choices. Consent must be informed and, under many laws, freely given and as easy to withdraw as to give.
Analogy. Security is the lock on the filing cabinet. Privacy asks whether that file should exist, who may read it, for what purpose, and when it must be shredded.

3. Europe: the GDPR (CIPP/E)

The EU (European Union)'s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, applied since May 2018) is the most influential privacy law. It applies to organisations in the EU and to those outside it that offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU. The UK has its own almost identical UK GDPR.

Principles (Article 5)

Lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability (you must be able to prove compliance).

Six lawful bases (Article 6)

Consent; contract; legal obligation; vital interests; public task; and legitimate interests (balanced against the person's rights).

Individuals' rights

To be informed; access; rectification; erasure ("right to be forgotten"); restriction; data portability; objection; and rights around automated decision-making. Organisations normally respond within one month.

Obligations

  • Controller decides purposes and means; processor acts on instructions under a written contract.
  • Data protection officer (DPO) required for public bodies and large-scale monitoring or sensitive processing.
  • DPIA (data protection impact assessment) for high-risk processing.
  • Records of processing activities.
  • Breach notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware, and to individuals when risk is high.
  • International transfers need an adequacy decision (such as the EU-US Data Privacy Framework for certified US companies), standard contractual clauses or binding corporate rules.
  • Fines up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

4. The United States and the rest of the world (CIPP/US)

The US has no single federal privacy law. Instead it has sector laws, a federal regulator, and fast-growing state laws.

LawCovers
HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)Health information held by providers, insurers and their business associates
GLBA (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act)Financial institutions' customer data
COPPAOnline data from children under 13
FERPAStudent education records
FCRAConsumer credit reports
FTC Act Section 5The Federal Trade Commission acts against unfair or deceptive practices, including broken privacy promises
State lawsCalifornia's CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) as amended by the CPRA (California Privacy Rights Act), and comprehensive laws in many other states; plus breach notification laws in every state

Elsewhere: Canada's PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act), Brazil's LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, Brazil's data protection law), China's PIPL, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) and many more. Most share GDPR-like principles but differ in details such as consent, data localisation and penalties.

Exam trap. HIPAA doesn't cover all health data, only data held by covered entities and their business associates. A fitness app selling direct to consumers is usually outside HIPAA (though the FTC and state laws may apply).

5. Running a privacy program (CIPM)

  1. Vision and governance: executive sponsor, privacy office, DPO or chief privacy officer, and a framework (for example the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) Privacy Framework or ISO/IEC (ISO: International Organization for Standardization; IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 27701).
  2. Know your data: a data inventory and data map showing what personal data you hold, where, why, who accesses it, and where it flows (including vendors and other countries).
  3. Policies: privacy notice, internal privacy policy, retention schedule, acceptable use.
  4. Assess: PIAs/DPIAs for new projects, vendor assessments and gap analyses against laws.
  5. Protect: work with security on controls; privacy by design in the development life cycle.
  6. Train staff by role.
  7. Respond: handle data subject requests on time; run a breach response plan with legal, security and communications.
  8. Sustain: metrics (request volumes and times, PIA completion, training rates), audits and continuous improvement.
The data inventory is the foundation. You can't answer an access request, assess a breach or prove compliance without knowing where personal data lives.

6. Privacy engineering (CDPSE)

TechniqueWhat it doesStill personal data?
Data minimisationCollect and keep only what the purpose needsN/A: less data to protect
PseudonymisationReplace identifiers with codes; the key is kept separatelyYes, because it can be re-identified with the key
AnonymisationIrreversibly remove the ability to identify anyoneNo, if truly irreversible (hard to achieve)
TokenisationSwap a value (such as a card number) for a meaningless tokenDepends who holds the vault
EncryptionMakes data unreadable without the keyYes, but a breach of encrypted data may not need notification
Aggregation, k-anonymity, differential privacyShare statistics while limiting what can be learned about any one personDesigned to prevent identification
  • Data lifecycle (CDPSE domain 3): collection, use, storage, sharing, retention and secure deletion. Automate deletion when retention ends.
  • Consent and preference management systems record what each person agreed to.
  • Privacy architecture (domain 2): access controls, logging, segregation of environments (no real personal data in testing), secure APIs (Application Programming Interface) and privacy-enhancing technologies.
  • AI (Artificial Intelligence) and privacy: training data, purpose limits, transparency about automated decisions, and new laws such as the EU AI Act (Module 30).

7. Getting into privacy

  • People arrive from law, compliance, security and IT (Information Technology). Technical people with privacy knowledge are especially scarce.
  • A common route: CIPP for your region (law), then CIPM (program) or CDPSE/CIPT (technology).
  • Pair it with security knowledge (Modules 16 to 18 and 20) and governance (Modules 32 and 34 to 36).
  • Follow your regulator's guidance (for example the European Data Protection Board, the UK Information Commissioner's Office or the US FTC); they publish practical examples.
