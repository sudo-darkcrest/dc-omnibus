Leading Security: Information Security Management

Why this matters. At some point a security career moves from fixing problems to deciding which problems matter, getting the money to solve them and leading the response when things go wrong. ISACA (Information Systems Audit and Control Association)'s Certified Information Security Manager (CISM) tests that management view. Its questions reward the answer a sensible manager would give: aligned with the business, backed by senior management, following governance and risk, not the most technical fix. This module teaches that mindset and ends with a 75-question CISM-style practice exam.

You will be able to

  • Describe the four CISM domains and their exam weights.
  • Build a security strategy aligned to business goals, with a business case and roadmap.
  • Run risk assessment and treatment from a manager's point of view, including ownership, reporting and emerging risks.
  • Develop and run a security program: resources, frameworks, policies, awareness, metrics and vendors.
  • Prepare for and manage incidents: IR (Incident Response) plans, BIA (Business Impact Analysis), BCP (Business Continuity Plan), DRP (Disaster Recovery Plan), testing, escalation, communication, forensics and lessons learned.
  • Answer "best / first / most" questions the way ISACA expects.

2. Think like a manager

Most CISM questions have two or three answers that would work. The best answer is the one a senior security manager should choose. Use these filters, in order:

  1. Business first. Does the answer support business objectives? Security enables the business.
  2. Governance and senior management. Does it follow policy, involve the right owner and keep leadership informed?
  3. Risk-based. Is it proportionate to risk and impact? Assess before acting.
  4. Process before technology. Strategy → policy → standards → controls → tools.
  5. Do the step that has to come first. "FIRST" questions usually reward understanding the situation, checking the plan, or finding the owner, before doing anything.
Word in the questionWhat it usually wants
FIRSTThe step that must happen before the others: assess, understand objectives, consult the plan, identify the owner
BEST / MOSTThe most complete, business-aligned, sustainable answer, often the one that addresses the root cause or governance
PRIMARY / MAINThe fundamental purpose, not a side benefit
GREATEST concern / riskThe issue with the largest business impact, often a governance gap (no owner, no support, no plan)
Choosing "install a better tool" or "fix it yourself." CISM rarely rewards jumping to technology or acting outside your role. A manager assesses, involves owners and follows governance.

3. Domain 1: Information security governance

Culture, structures and roles

  • Board: sets risk appetite, oversees. Senior management: approves strategy, policy and resources. Steering committee: aligns priorities across departments. CISO (Chief Information Security Officer) / information security manager: develops and runs the program, advises. Data owners: classify and accept risk for their data. Custodians: implement controls. Users: follow policy.
  • The CISO should report high enough to be heard and independent of IT (Information Technology) operations where possible, to avoid conflicts of interest.
  • A healthy security culture is one where people report mistakes and suspicious activity without fear.

Legal, regulatory and contractual requirements

Identify obligations with legal counsel (privacy laws, breach notification, sector rules such as HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) or PCI (Payment Card Industry) DSS (Data Security Standard), contracts), map them to controls and monitor changes. Where requirements overlap, build one control set that satisfies many ("comply once, report many").

Strategy, business cases and roadmaps

  • Desired state can be described with frameworks (COBIT (Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies) capability levels, NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) target profiles, ISO (International Organization for Standardization) 27001 controls) and with business attributes ("customers trust us with payment data").
  • Business case: problem, options, costs, benefits (risk reduction, compliance, enablement), risks of doing nothing, alignment with objectives.
  • Roadmap: phased initiatives prioritized by risk, dependencies, quick wins and budget cycles.
  • Governance frameworks used: COBIT 2019, ISO/IEC (IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 27001/27002, NIST CSF 2.0 (see Module 26).

4. Domain 2: Information security risk management

  • Risk assessment: identify assets and their value (set by the owner), threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood and impact; analyse qualitatively or quantitatively (SLE (Single Loss Expectancy), ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy); Module 26).
  • Threat and vulnerability management: threat intelligence, vulnerability scanning, prioritization by business impact, not just CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System).
  • Risk treatment and ownership: the risk owner (a business manager) chooses to mitigate, transfer, avoid or accept within appetite. The security manager advises, facilitates and reports.
  • Control selection: proportionate to risk, cost-effective, aligned with frameworks; use compensating controls and documented exceptions when the standard control isn't possible.
  • Monitoring and reporting: risk register, KRIs (Key Risk Indicator), trend reporting to management; reassess when the business, technology or threats change.
  • Emerging risks: AI (Artificial Intelligence) misuse and data leakage, supply chain attacks, quantum threats to cryptography, new regulations. Assess and govern before buying tools.
ISACA's view: risk management is a continuous cycle, and the information security manager's main job is making sure risk owners have the information to make good decisions.

5. Domain 3: The information security program

This is the biggest domain (about a third of the exam). The program turns strategy into results.

6. Domain 4: Incident management

Readiness

  • Incident response plan (IRP): scope, definitions, severity classes, roles (incident manager, technical leads, legal, HR (Human Resources), communications), escalation criteria, contact lists, playbooks, evidence handling; approved by senior management.
  • BIA: identifies critical processes and how impact grows over time; sets RTO (Recovery Time Objective), RPO (Recovery Point Objective) and MTD (Maximum Tolerable Downtime).
  • BCP keeps the business running (people, sites, manual workarounds); DRP restores IT (sites, backups, replication).
  • Recovery sites: hot (ready in hours), warm (days), cold (space and power, weeks), mirrored/active-active, cloud DR (Disaster Recovery).
  • Testing: checklist review → tabletop → walkthrough → simulation → parallel → full interruption (risk and realism increase).

Operations

  • Classify and escalate using predefined severity criteria based on business impact.
  • Communicate through defined channels: internal stakeholders, executives, a single spokesperson for the media, customers and regulators as required by law and contract (legal counsel decides notification).
  • Forensics coordination: preserve evidence before rebuilding, keep chain of custody, involve legal; consider law enforcement.
  • Post-incident review: root cause, what worked, what to improve; update plans, controls, training. No blame.

7. Thinking like a security manager: three scenarios

CISM questions are about judgement, not configuration. The best answer usually aligns security with business goals and follows due process. Try these.

Scenario 1. The sales director wants to let staff use personal phones for email immediately. The security team thinks it is risky. What should the security manager do FIRST?

Best answer: Assess the risk and present options (for example, mobile device management with containerised email) so the business owner can make an informed decision. Security advises; the business owner decides and accepts the risk.
Scenario 2. A new regulation requires encryption of customer data within 12 months. What is the BEST first step?

Best answer: Perform a gap analysis against the requirement to find what is not already compliant, then build a prioritised plan with owners and budget.
Scenario 3. After a major incident, the CEO asks why the incident response plan didn't work. What should the security manager have ensured beforehand?

Best answer: That the plan was tested regularly (tabletop and technical exercises), updated after each test, and that roles and authority were agreed by senior management.
CISM answer patterns: prefer governance and risk-based decisions over technical fixes, involve senior management and business owners, and choose the answer that comes first in a logical process (assess before implementing).

8. Reporting security to the board

Boards don't want packet counts. They want to know whether risk is within appetite and whether security spending is working. Good security metrics are tied to business outcomes.

Weak metricBetter metricWhy it's better
Number of firewall blocksPercentage of critical systems with known-exploited vulnerabilities patched within 14 daysShows exposure to attacks that actually happen
Phishing emails receivedPercentage of staff who report simulated phishing, and how fastMeasures behaviour that reduces risk
Number of policiesPercentage of critical risks with an owner and treatment plan on trackShows governance working
Antivirus installed countMean time to detect and contain incidentsShows operational capability
A board report is like a car dashboard: a few clear gauges (speed, fuel, warning lights), not a printout of every sensor reading in the engine.
