Artificial Intelligence: How It Works, What It Can and Can't Do, and Where It Might Lead

Why this matters. AI is now built into phones, search engines, office apps, security tools and help desks, and it is the main reason new data centers are being built (Module 23). You'll be asked to use it, support it, secure it and explain it. This module takes the mystery out: what is actually happening inside an AI model, why it is brilliant at some things and unreliable at others, and what the next few years might bring.

You will be able to

  • Explain the difference between AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI.
  • Describe how a model learns from data, and how a large language model produces text one token at a time.
  • Judge which tasks AI does well, which need human checking, and which it can't do.
  • Name the main AI risks (hallucination, bias, prompt injection, privacy) and the frameworks that govern AI.
  • Discuss where AI might lead, separating evidence from speculation, and pick an AI certification.

1. What AI is (and isn't)

Artificial intelligence is any computer system doing a task we'd normally say needs human intelligence: recognising faces, translating languages, answering questions, spotting fraud. Most modern AI is machine learning: instead of a programmer writing every rule, the computer learns patterns from examples.

YearMilestone
1950Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the imitation game (Turing test)
1956The term "artificial intelligence" is coined at the Dartmouth workshop
1970s–90s"AI winters": funding dries up when early promises aren't met; expert systems rise and fall
1997IBM Deep Blue beats chess champion Garry Kasparov

2016DeepMind's AlphaGo beats Go champion Lee Sedol

2022ChatGPT launches and reaches 100 million users in about two months; generative AI goes mainstream

"AI" is not one thing, and it is not a person. A spam filter, a self-driving car's vision system and a chatbot are all AI but work very differently. Today's systems are narrow: very capable at what they were trained for, with no feelings or intentions.

2. How machine learning learns

Imagine teaching a child to tell cats from dogs. You don't give them a rulebook; you show them lots of pictures and correct their mistakes. Machine learning works the same way, but with maths:

  1. Data: collect examples, such as 10,000 emails, each labelled "spam" or "not spam".
  2. Model: start with a model full of adjustable numbers called weights, set randomly at first.
  3. Predict and measure: the model guesses; a loss function scores how wrong it was.
  4. Adjust: gradient descent nudges every weight slightly in the direction that reduces the error.
  5. Repeat millions of times. Then test on examples the model has never seen, to check it learned the idea and didn't just memorise (overfitting).

Supervised

Learn from labelled examples. Spam filters, medical image screening, price prediction.

Unsupervised

Find structure without labels. Grouping customers, spotting unusual network traffic (anomaly detection).

Reinforcement

Learn by trial and error with rewards. Game playing, robotics, and fine-tuning chatbots with human feedback (RLHF).

3. How large language models work

A large language model (LLM), the kind behind chatbots, is a huge neural network trained on a vast amount of text. Its core skill is surprisingly simple: predict the next token (a word or piece of a word). Do that well enough, at enormous scale, and grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and coding styles all emerge as side effects.

  1. Tokenise: your text is cut into tokens. "Unbelievable!" might become "Un", "believ", "able", "!".
  2. Embed: each token becomes a long list of numbers (an embedding) that captures meaning. Similar words get similar numbers.
  3. Attention: stacked transformer layers let every token "look at" every other token and decide which matter. In "The bank by the river flooded", attention links "bank" to "river", not money.
  4. Predict: the model outputs a probability for every possible next token. "The capital of France is" → "Paris" 92%, "a" 3%, …
  5. Sample and repeat: one token is picked (the temperature setting controls how adventurous the pick is), added to the text, and the whole process runs again for the next token.
StageWhat happens
Pre-trainingMonths on thousands of GPUs predicting the next token across a huge text collection. Produces a "base model" that can continue text but isn't a good assistant yet.
Fine-tuning and alignmentFurther training on example conversations, plus feedback from people or AI (RLHF and similar), to follow instructions, be helpful and refuse harmful requests.
InferenceRunning the finished model for each user request. Cheaper per request than training, but it happens billions of times a day.
Retrieval (RAG) and toolsAt answer time the system can search documents or the web, run code or call APIs (Application Programming Interface), and put the results in the prompt so answers are current and grounded.
An LLM is like an extremely well-read improviser. It has absorbed the patterns of millions of books and conversations and can continue almost any text convincingly. But improvisers don't check a reference book mid-sentence. Unless it's given sources, it says what sounds right, which is usually but not always what is right.

4. The machines behind AI

AI is maths: trillions of multiplications. GPUs (and accelerators such as Google TPUs, AWS (Amazon Web Services) Trainium and Inferentia) do thousands of these in parallel, using very fast stacked memory called HBM (High Bandwidth Memory). Training a frontier model can use tens of thousands of GPUs linked by very fast networks for months.

Power

An AI rack can draw 40 to 130+ kW, compared with 5 to 10 kW for a typical server rack. New AI campuses are planned at hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts (verify current figures).

Cooling

Air can't remove that much heat, so direct-to-chip liquid cooling and rear-door heat exchangers are becoming standard (Module 23).

On your device

Smaller models run on phones and laptops using an NPU (Neural Processing Unit) (Module 4), which keeps data private and works offline.

5. What AI can and can't do

Usually good atNeeds careful checkingCan't do, or shouldn't be trusted to
Drafting and rewriting text, summarising long documentsFacts, figures, quotes and citations (it can hallucinate)Guarantee it's correct, or know when it's wrong
Translating, explaining concepts at any levelMaths and multi-step logic (better with tools, still slips)Know events after its training cutoff without search tools
Writing and explaining code, finding likely bugsCode that will run in production (security flaws, invented libraries)Take legal, medical or financial responsibility
Recognising patterns in images, audio and logsAnything involving people's rights (hiring, lending, policing): bias riskTruly understand or care about you; it has no feelings or goals of its own
Brainstorming and first drafts at great speedAdvice for your specific situationKeep secrets you paste into a public tool (check the provider's data policy)
A safe rule for using AI at work: use it to go faster, not to stop thinking. Give it context, ask for sources, check anything that matters, never paste confidential data into a tool your company hasn't approved, and stay accountable for the result.

6. Risks, security and rules

RiskExampleMitigation
HallucinationInvented legal cases in a court filingRAG with citations, human review, low temperature for facts
Bias and unfairnessA CV (Curriculum Vitae, a résumé) screener favouring one group because past hires didDiverse data, fairness testing, human oversight
Prompt injectionHidden text in an email tells an AI assistant to forward your inboxTreat inputs as untrusted, least-privilege tools, human approval for actions
Data leakage and privacyStaff paste customer data into a public chatbotApproved enterprise tools, data loss prevention, clear policy
Data poisoning and model theftAttackers tamper with training data or copy a model through its APISupply-chain controls, access control, rate limits, monitoring
Deepfakes and AI-powered fraudA cloned voice of the CFO asks for an urgent wire transferCall-back verification, out-of-band approval, awareness training

Frameworks and laws: the OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Top 10 for LLM Applications lists the main technical risks; the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) guides organisations; ISO/IEC (ISO: International Organization for Standardization; IEC: International Electrotechnical Commission) 42001 is a certifiable AI management system; and the EU (European Union) AI Act sorts AI into unacceptable (banned, e.g., social scoring), high-risk (e.g., hiring, credit, critical infrastructure), limited-risk (transparency duties, e.g., chatbots must say they're AI) and minimal-risk uses, with obligations phased in from 2025 to 2027 (verify dates).

7. Where AI might lead

Nobody knows exactly, and confident predictions in AI have often been wrong in both directions. Here's what is already happening and what is possible but uncertain:

AreaHappening nowPossible next (speculative)
AgentsAI that browses, writes and runs code, and completes multi-step tasks with toolsAgents trusted to run whole workflows with light supervision; new security and accountability problems
WorkFaster writing, coding, support and analysis; some tasks automatedMany jobs change shape rather than vanish; new roles in AI operations, governance and security; disruption for some roles
Science and healthProtein structure prediction (AlphaFold, 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), drug discovery, medical imaging supportFaster research cycles; AI as a lab partner
InfrastructureRecord data center construction, power and cooling constraints, interest in nuclear and new energy supplyEnergy becomes the main limit on AI growth; more efficient chips and models
Everyday techAssistants built into phones, PCs (Personal Computer), cars and home devicesNatural conversation becomes a normal way to use computers
RulesEU AI Act, national AI safety institutes, company AI policiesMore regulation, audits and AI certifications (good news for GRC (Governance, Risk and Compliance) careers, Modules 26–28)
General AIExperts disagree on whether and when "artificial general intelligence" (human-level across most tasks) will arriveViews range from "within years" to "decades" to "not with current methods"; safety research tries to stay ahead
A useful habit: when you read an AI prediction, ask "what evidence is this based on, and who benefits if I believe it?" The same critical thinking you use for phishing emails (Module 16) works for hype.

9. Using AI tools well: a practical guide

AI assistants are now everyday tools at work. Using them well is a skill, and so is knowing when not to use them.

Getting better answers

  • Give context: who you are, what you are trying to do, and who the answer is for. "Explain subnetting to a beginner studying for Network+" beats "explain subnetting".
  • Provide the material: paste the error message, log lines or document (after removing secrets) instead of describing them.
  • Ask for a format: a table, a checklist, or a short answer first then details.
  • Iterate: treat the first answer as a draft. Ask it to check its own work, give sources, or try another approach.

Staying safe

  • Verify facts that matter, especially numbers, commands, legal points and citations. Models can state wrong things confidently.
  • Never paste secrets (passwords, keys, customer personal data) into tools your organisation has not approved.
  • Test commands in a lab before running them on real systems.
  • Follow your employer's AI policy: many organisations allow only approved tools for work data.
  • You are responsible for the result. "The AI wrote it" is not an excuse for an error in a report or a broken configuration.
A good study use: paste a practice question you got wrong and ask the assistant to explain why each wrong option is wrong. Then check the explanation against this Omnibus or the official objectives.

10. How AI is changing IT jobs

AI changes tasks before it changes whole jobs. In IT (Information Technology), the pattern so far looks like this.

AreaWhat AI increasingly helps withWhat still needs people
Help deskAnswering routine questions, drafting replies, suggesting fixes from past ticketsUnusual problems, upset users, hands-on hardware work
Programming and scriptingWriting first drafts of code and tests, explaining unfamiliar codeDesign decisions, review, security and accountability
Security operationsSummarising alerts, correlating logs, drafting incident reportsJudgement on real incidents, containment decisions, legal and business calls
Networking and cloudGenerating configurations, spotting anomalies, answering documentation questionsArchitecture, change approval, troubleshooting physical faults
Governance and auditMapping controls to frameworks, drafting policies, reviewing evidenceProfessional judgement, sign-off, ethics

People who understand the fundamentals benefit most, because they can tell a good AI suggestion from a bad one. That is a strong reason to keep learning the basics in this Omnibus, not a reason to skip them.

Calculators did not remove the need to understand maths. They removed tedious arithmetic and made people who understood maths much faster.
